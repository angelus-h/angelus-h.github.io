    Alternative Photography Processes for Home Studio Practice

    A Technical Guide to Cyanotype, Salt Print, and Gelatin Silver Contact Printing

    Last Updated: 2026-06-01

    Introduction

    This guide provides technically accurate, practice-oriented information about three historical photographic processes suitable for home studio work with A4-sized prints. All information is verified against manufacturer specifications, conservation research, practitioner communities, and contemporary alternative photography resources.

    Target Audience: - Photographers transitioning from analog/digital to alternative processes - Fine art printmakers exploring historical techniques - Artists preparing work for online sales (Etsy, galleries) - Small-scale studio practitioners with limited space

    Design Philosophy: - A4 format contact printing (scalable to A3) - Minimal water and chemical usage - Low-toxicity workflow where possible - Fine art / limited edition focus - Atmospheric, melancholic imagery emphasis

    Part 1: Cyanotype

    1.1 Overview

    What is Cyanotype?

    Cyanotype is a photographic printing process that produces cyan-blue prints. Invented by Sir John Herschel in 1842, it uses iron salts sensitive to UV light to create the distinctive Prussian blue image.3 Popularized by Anna Atkins in her 1843 botanical documentation Photographs of British Algae, it remains the most accessible alternative process for beginners.4

    Visual Character: - Vibrant Prussian blue color - Sharp detail with visible paper texture - High clarity, somewhat high contrast (~grade 3) - Tendency to block up shadow detail - Best suited for: botanical subjects, landscapes with open skies, graphic compositions

    Difficulty: Beginner-friendly
    Toxicity: Low (ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide are irritants, not highly toxic)
    Water Usage: Low (~500ml per A4 print for washing)
    Archival Stability: Extremely archival when stored in unbuffered, pH-neutral materials5

    1.2 Minimal Starter Setup

    Essential Equipment: - 2 small amber glass bottles (50-100ml each) for storing sensitizer solutions - 2 foam brushes or glass coating rods - Contact printing frame (A4 size) or heavy glass sheet + backing board - Plastic trays (3x A4 size minimum) - Measuring cups/syringes - Rubber gloves - UV light source (sunlight or UV LED box)

    Initial Investment: ~$50-100 USD

    1.3 Complete Shopping List

    Chemicals

    Pre-Mixed Kit Option (Easiest): - Bostick & Sullivan Cyanotype Printing Kit (250ml kit, ~200 prints @ 8x10", $18.95-$199.99 depending on size)6 - Contains 25% Ferric Ammonium Citrate (Solution A) - Contains 10% Potassium Ferricyanide (Solution B)

    DIY Mixing Option (More Economical Long-Term): - Ferric Ammonium Citrate (green sensitive) - 100g - Potassium Ferricyanide - 50g - Available from: Bostick & Sullivan, Photographer's Formulary, chemistry suppliers

    Water: - Distilled water for mixing solutions - Tap water acceptable for washing (unless extremely hard)

    Papers

    Budget Starter Papers: - Canson XL Watercolor Paper (140lb/300gsm cold press) - widely available, affordable (~$0.30/sheet) - Strathmore 400 Series Watercolor (140lb cold press) - similar price point

    Fine Art Papers (Recommended):

    According to the AlternativePhotography.com paper survey, the most popular papers for cyanotype are:7

    • Arches Platine - Premium choice, prominent texture, bright whites (~$4.62/sheet for 25+ orders, though pricing varies)
    • Rives BFK - Smooth texture, excellent value, "pretty inexpensive"8
    • Fabriano Artistico - 100% cotton, good absorption
    • Arches Aquarelle Cold Press 140lb - Textured surface, widely used
    • Hahnemühle Platinum Rag - Smooth, high-quality cotton

    Paper Characteristics for Cyanotype: - 100% cotton rag papers produce the deepest blues - Cold press (textured) surface creates visible paper texture in prints - Hot press (smooth) gives smoother tonal transitions - 140lb (300gsm) weight is standard - heavier papers (300lb) resist curling - CRITICAL: Use unbuffered or acid-free papers only - buffered papers fade cyanotype blues over time

    Budget vs Fine Art:

    Rives BFK ~$1.50-2.50 Excellent value for editions Smooth, consistent, professional results

    Fabriano Artistico ~$2.00-4.00 Limited edition prints Very good blue density, classic watercolor texture

    Equipment

    Essential: - Contact printing frame (A4 size) - $30-60 USD - Alternative: 3mm glass sheet + foam board backing + binder clips (~$15-20) - 3x plastic developing trays (A4 size) - $20-30 - Foam brushes (pack of 10) - $8-12 - Glass coating rod (optional, for even coating) - $15-25 - Amber glass bottles (2x 100ml) - $10-15 - Rubber gloves (box) - $8-12 - Measuring cups/syringes - $10-15

    UV Light Source Options:

    1. Sunlight (Free):
    2. Exposure time: 15-30 minutes depending on season/latitude
    3. Unpredictable, weather-dependent9

    4. Excellent for testing and learning

    5. DIY UV LED Box:

    6. 30W UV LED floodlight (365nm) suspended 30cm above paper10
    7. Exposure time: 5-10 minutes
    8. Cost: $25-50 USD

    9. Consistent, weather-independent results

    10. Commercial UV Exposure Unit:

    11. Dedicated UV box with timer
    12. Cost: $200-500+ USD
    13. Professional consistency

    Recommended for Beginners: Start with sunlight for testing, then build a $30-50 DIY UV LED box for consistent production work.

    1.4 Paper Recommendations

    For Testing and Experimentation: - Canson XL Watercolor 140lb cold press (affordable, widely available) - Strathmore 400 Series Watercolor

    For Fine Art / Limited Editions: - Rives BFK - Best value, smooth surface, professional results - Arches Platine - Premium choice for museum-quality prints - Fabriano Artistico - Classic watercolor paper, excellent blue density

    Buffered vs Non-Buffered:

    CRITICAL CONSIDERATION: Cyanotype chemistry is iron-based and acidic. Buffered papers (containing calcium carbonate) will gradually fade the blue color over time. Always use: - Unbuffered papers, or - Acid-free papers explicitly tested for cyanotype

    Most fine art watercolor papers (Arches, Fabriano, Rives BFK) are suitable, but verify with manufacturer specifications.

    1.5 Contact Printing Workflow (A4 Size)

    Step 1: Prepare Digital Negative

    Software: - Adobe Photoshop, GIMP, or Affinity Photo - Convert image to black & white - Adjust contrast (cyanotype has ~3 stops dynamic range - increase contrast slightly) - Invert the image (black becomes clear, white becomes black) - Mirror/flip horizontally (image will reverse during printing) - Resize to A4 (210 x 297mm)

    Printing the Negative:

    For A4 home printing, use:11

    • Film: Inkjet transparency film (OHP film)
    • Cyanoprints.com A4 Inkjet Transparencies (50 sheets, designed for cyanotype)12
    • Office Depot/Office supply generic transparencies work adequately

    • Pictorico OHP Ultra Premium (professional choice, higher density)

    • Printer: Inkjet printer with pigment-based black ink (NOT dye-based)

    • Epson EcoTank series (budget option)
    • Epson P700/P900 (professional option, see note below)
    • Canon PIXMA Pro series

    Epson P800/P900 "Pizza Wheel" Issue:

    If using Epson P800 or P900 printers, be aware of the "pizza wheel" marking problem:13

    • Default rear feed causes roller marks on transparencies
    • Solutions:
    • Use front loading mode (disengages pizza rollers) + tape transparency to poster board14
    • Increase drying time from 0 to 10 in printer settings (slower but minimizes marks)
    • European firmware update enables "no eject roller" option
    • Use QuadToneRIP software for better ink control

    Printer Settings: - Media type: "OHP Transparency" or "Premium Matte Presentation Paper" - Print quality: Highest available - Color management: None (print as grayscale)

    Step 2: Coat the Paper

    Lighting: Work under normal room light (cyanotype chemistry is not light-sensitive until mixed)

    1. Mix equal parts Solution A (ferric ammonium citrate) and Solution B (potassium ferricyanide)
    2. Mix only what you need - combined solution has ~24 hour working life

    3. For A4 size: ~8-10ml total (4-5ml each solution)

    4. Coat the paper evenly:

    5. Use foam brush or glass rod
    6. Coat in dim light (not critical, but helps see coverage)
    7. Brush/spread evenly across paper surface
    8. Aim for smooth, streak-free coating
    9. Allow to dry completely in darkness (15-30 minutes in dry climate, longer in humid conditions)

    Tips: - Coated paper is now light-sensitive - store in darkness until exposure - Coated paper can be stored for weeks in a dark, dry environment - Coating under low humidity produces better results

    Step 3: Exposure

    1. Place dried, coated paper in contact frame (coated side up)
    2. Place digital negative on top (emulsion side DOWN, touching the coated paper)
    3. Close frame or add glass + weight to ensure tight contact
    4. Expose to UV light:
    5. Sunlight: 15-30 minutes (watch for color shift from yellow-green to gray-blue)
    6. UV LED box: 5-10 minutes (test your specific setup)
    7. Proper exposure shows visible image with dark blue-gray highlights before washing

    Judging Exposure: - Underexposed: Pale blue after washing, weak image - Properly exposed: Deep Prussian blue, strong image density - Overexposed: Very dark, blocked shadows (less common problem)

    Step 4: Washing and Finishing

    1. First rinse: Gently rinse print under running water (cold tap water is fine)
    2. Image will appear muddy yellow-green at first
    3. Continue rinsing until water runs clear

    4. Blue color will deepen dramatically during washing

    5. Washing tray: Soak print in tray of clean water for 5-10 minutes

    6. Change water 2-3 times

    7. Total water usage: ~500ml per print

    8. Optional oxidation bath: Soak in dilute hydrogen peroxide (3%, available at pharmacy) for 1-2 minutes

    9. Deepens blue color and speeds oxidation

    10. Not required - air oxidation will achieve same result in 24 hours

    11. Drying: Hang or lay flat to dry

    12. Blue will continue to deepen over 24-48 hours as oxidation completes
    13. Avoid direct sunlight during drying (though cyanotypes are light-stable once dry)

    1.8 What Makes Cyanotype Ideal for Etsy Fine Art?

    Market Positioning:

    Cyanotype has strong appeal for Etsy and online fine art sales:15

    • Distinctive aesthetic: Prussian blue is instantly recognizable, unique from digital prints
    • Botanical and nature subjects: Align perfectly with Etsy's handmade/natural goods audience
    • Story and provenance: Historical process (1842) + handmade narrative adds value
    • Archival quality: Extremely stable when stored properly - legitimate fine art medium
    • Limited edition potential: Each print is hand-coated, justifying limited series pricing
    • Low overhead: Enables profitable pricing even at modest scales

    Etsy Pricing Research (2026):

    Based on current Etsy market analysis: - Small cyanotypes (5x7", 8x10"): $25-60 USD - Medium cyanotypes (11x14", A4): $60-150 USD - Large cyanotypes (16x20", A3): $150-400 USD - Original one-of-a-kind prints command 50-100% premium over editions

    Successful Artist Examples:

    • Oriana Poindexter - Sells cyanotype, salt print, and silver gelatin prints as archival fine art

    What Sells Well: - Botanical subjects (ferns, leaves, flowers) - Coastal/marine themes (seaweed, shells, driftwood) - Architectural details - Abstract organic forms - Limited edition series (numbered, signed)

    Print Presentation: - Sign and number prints - Include certificate of authenticity - Use archival matting and backing - Describe process and materials in listing - Emphasize handmade, one-of-a-kind nature

    Part 2: Salt Print (Salted Paper Print)

    2.1 Overview

    What is Salt Print?

    Salt printing, invented by William Henry Fox Talbot in the mid-1830s, was the dominant paper-based photographic process from 1839 to approximately 1860.16 The process involves two steps: first salting paper with sodium chloride (table salt), then sensitizing with silver nitrate to create a UV-sensitive surface.

    Visual Character: - Warm brown to purple-brown tones (depending on toning) - Soft, matte appearance - Image embedded in paper fibers (not on surface layer) - Long tonal scale with delicate highlights and smooth shadows - Extremely low contrast compared to cyanotype17 - Best suited for: portraits, soft landscapes, vintage aesthetic, fine art prints

    Difficulty: Intermediate (requires two-step coating, careful silver nitrate handling)
    Toxicity: Moderate (silver nitrate is corrosive and stains skin)
    Water Usage: Moderate (~1L per A4 print)
    Archival Stability: Requires gold or selenium toning for long-term stability18

    2.2 Minimal Starter Setup

    Essential Equipment: - 2 small amber glass bottles (100ml each) for salt solution and silver nitrate - Foam brushes or glass coating rods - Contact printing frame (A4 size) or glass + backing - Plastic trays (4x A4 size minimum) - Safelight (7-watt yellow/red bulb) or work in dim room - Fixer (sodium thiosulfate) - Toning solution (gold or selenium toner) - Rubber gloves (mandatory - silver nitrate stains skin black) - Protective eyewear

    Initial Investment: ~$80-150 USD

    SAFETY NOTE: Silver nitrate is corrosive and permanently stains skin. Always wear gloves and eye protection.

    2.3 Complete Shopping List

    Chemicals

    Pre-Mixed Kit Option (Recommended for Beginners):

    • Bostick & Sullivan 100ml Salted Paper Printing Kit19
    • Includes pre-mixed salting solution, silver nitrate sensitizer, fixer
    • Full instructions included
    • Price: Contact Bostick & Sullivan (505-474-0890)

    DIY Mixing Option:

    Salting Solution: - Sodium chloride (table salt, non-iodized) - 20g per liter of distilled water (2% solution)20 - Optional: Gelatin or arrowroot starch for sizing (improves surface quality)

    Sensitizer: - Silver nitrate (AgNO₃) - 50-100g - Distilled water

    Fixer: - Sodium thiosulfate (hypo) - 250g - Distilled water

    Toning (Essential for Archival Prints): - Gold toner (sodium gold chloride) - recommended for warm tones and archival stability - Selenium toner - alternative for cooler tones - Borax (optional, for gold toner bath)

    Papers

    Salt prints work best on papers with minimal sizing, allowing the silver solution to penetrate the fibers:21

    Recommended Papers: - Rives BFK - Smooth, unsized, excellent for salt printing - Arches Platine - Premium choice, minimal sizing - Fabriano Artistico - Good absorption, traditional watercolor paper - Hahnemühle Platinum Rag - Smooth, high-quality cotton - Bergger COT-320 - Specifically designed for alternative processes

    Paper Characteristics for Salt Print: - Unsized or lightly sized papers produce softer, more embedded images - Heavily sized papers create more surface-layer images with slight sheen - 100% cotton rag papers are traditional and archival - Hot press (smooth) works better than cold press (textured) for salt prints

    Equipment

    Same basic equipment as cyanotype, with additions:

    Essential: - Contact frame (A4) - $30-60 - 4x plastic trays (A4 size) - $30-40 - Foam brushes - $8-12 - Amber glass bottles (2-3x 100ml) - $15-20 - Rubber gloves (thick, chemical-resistant) - $12-20 - Protective eyewear - $10-15 - Safelight (7W yellow or red bulb) - $15-25 or work in dim tungsten light at distance22 - Graduated cylinders for measuring - $15-25 - Thermometer (for developer/fixer temperatures) - $10-15

    UV Light Source: - Same options as cyanotype (sunlight, UV LED box, commercial unit) - Exposure times: 15-60 minutes depending on light source and negative density

    2.4 Paper Recommendations

    For Testing: - Canson XL Watercolor (140lb hot press if available) - Strathmore 400 Series (smooth)

    For Fine Art Prints: - Rives BFK - Industry favorite for salt printing, smooth, unsized - Arches Platine - Premium archival quality - Bergger COT-320 - Specifically formulated for alternative processes

    Sizing Considerations:

    Unlike cyanotype, salt prints benefit from minimal sizing: - Unsized papers: Silver solution soaks into fibers → soft, matte, deeply embedded image - Sized papers: Silver stays more on surface → slight sheen, different tonality (ruddy to purple)

    Most printmakers prefer unsized or lightly sized papers for the characteristic "embedded" salt print aesthetic.

    2.5 Contact Printing Workflow (A4 Size)

    Step 1: Prepare Digital Negative

    Same process as cyanotype (see Section 1.5, Step 1), with one important difference:

    • Salt prints have a long tonal scale and low contrast23
    • DO NOT increase contrast aggressively like cyanotype
    • Adjust for slightly higher contrast than the original image, but much less than cyanotype
    • Test exposure and adjust digital negative contrast accordingly

    Use the same transparency film and printer workflow as cyanotype.

    Step 2: Salt the Paper

    Lighting: Work under normal room light (salting step is not light-sensitive)

    1. Prepare 2% salt solution:
    2. 20g non-iodized table salt
    3. 1 liter distilled water

    4. Mix thoroughly until dissolved

    5. Pour solution into shallow tray (A4 size or larger)

    6. Float or soak paper in salt solution for 2-3 minutes

    7. Ensure even coverage

    8. Flip paper if needed

    9. Hang paper to dry completely

    10. Drying time: 30-60 minutes depending on humidity
    11. Salted paper can be stored indefinitely in dry conditions

    Note: Some practitioners add gelatin or arrowroot starch to the salting solution for improved surface quality and tonal characteristics. This is optional for beginners.

    Step 3: Sensitize the Paper

    Lighting: Work under safelight (7W yellow/red bulb at 1 meter distance) or dim tungsten light24

    CRITICAL SAFETY: Wear thick rubber gloves and eye protection. Silver nitrate is corrosive and stains skin black permanently.

    1. Prepare silver nitrate sensitizer:
    2. 10-12% solution is standard (100-120g silver nitrate per liter distilled water)

    3. Start with 12% for denser negatives, 10% for thinner negatives

    4. Coat the dried salted paper with silver nitrate solution:

    5. Use foam brush or glass rod
    6. Coat evenly in dim safelight conditions

    7. Work relatively quickly - paper is now light-sensitive

    8. Dry the sensitized paper in complete darkness

    9. Drying time: 15-30 minutes
    10. Use immediately after drying - sensitized paper degrades within hours

    Tips: - Sensitize paper shortly before exposure (same day) - Store sensitized paper in dark, dry environment if not using immediately - Paper will have a pale yellow-green color when sensitized

    Step 4: Exposure

    Same process as cyanotype:

    1. Place dried, sensitized paper in contact frame (sensitized side up)
    2. Place digital negative on top (emulsion side down, in contact)
    3. Close frame to ensure tight contact
    4. Expose to UV light:
    5. Sunlight: 15-60 minutes (longer than cyanotype - salt prints print more slowly)
    6. UV LED box: 10-20 minutes (test your specific setup)
    7. Image should be clearly visible with dark reddish-brown color before processing

    Judging Exposure: - Properly exposed: Visible image with warm reddish-brown color, good shadow detail - Underexposed: Pale, weak image after fixing - Overexposed: Very dark, muddy image (less common - salt prints are forgiving)

    Step 5: Fixing

    Lighting: Work under safelight or dim room light

    1. Prepare fixer bath:
    2. Sodium thiosulfate (hypo): 20% solution (200g per liter distilled water)

    3. Temperature: Room temperature (20°C/68°F)

    4. Immerse print in fixer for 5-10 minutes

    5. Image will lighten and stabilize

    6. Gently agitate tray occasionally

    7. CRITICAL: Fix until all unexposed silver is removed

    8. Incomplete fixing leads to print degradation over time

    Step 6: Washing

    1. Rinse print under running water for 2-3 minutes

    2. Wash in tray of fresh water for 20-30 minutes

    3. Change water 3-4 times

    4. Total water usage: ~1L per A4 print

    5. Final rinse under running water for 2-3 minutes

    Water Quality: - Distilled or deionized water is ideal for final washes - Tap water is acceptable if not heavily chlorinated or hard

    Step 7: Toning (Essential for Archival Stability)

    WHY TONING IS CRITICAL:

    Untoned salt prints are sensitive to light and will fade over time. Gold or selenium toning converts the silver image to a more stable compound, ensuring archival longevity.25

    Gold Toning (Recommended):

    1. Prepare gold toner bath:
    2. Sodium gold chloride: 1% solution
    3. Borax: Optional addition for warmer tones

    4. Dilute as per manufacturer instructions (varies by product)

    5. Immerse washed print in gold toner for 5-10 minutes

    6. Image will shift to warmer purple-brown tones

    7. Increases archival stability significantly

    8. Wash again for 10-15 minutes in fresh water

    Selenium Toning (Alternative):

    • Produces cooler brown tones
    • Also provides archival protection
    • Follow manufacturer dilution instructions (typically 1:9 to 1:20)

    Step 8: Final Drying

    Hang or lay flat to dry completely. Salt prints are fully processed and archival (if toned properly) once dry.

    Density: - Salt prints require denser negatives than cyanotype - Increase ink density or print twice on same transparency if using inkjet negatives

    Part 3: Gelatin Silver Contact Printing

    3.1 Overview

    What is Gelatin Silver Contact Printing?

    Gelatin silver printing is the traditional black-and-white darkroom process, developed in the 1870s-1880s and refined throughout the 20th century. It uses light-sensitive silver halides embedded in a gelatin emulsion coated on paper. Contact printing refers to making prints the same size as the negative by placing them in direct contact during exposure (rather than enlarging in a darkroom enlarger).28

    Visual Character: - True neutral black tones (no color cast) - Maximum sharpness and detail resolution - Longest tonal range of all analog processes - Surface can be glossy, semi-gloss, or matte depending on paper - Deep blacks, brilliant whites, smooth gradations - Best suited for: Fine art photography, classic black & white imagery, maximum image quality

    Difficulty: Intermediate (requires full darkroom or changing bag + chemistry knowledge)
    Toxicity: Moderate to High (developer contains hydroquinone/metol, fixer contains acetic acid)
    Water Usage: High (~2-5L per A4 print, depending on washer efficiency)
    Archival Stability: Highest archival stability when properly processed and washed29

    3.5 Contact Printing Workflow (A4 Size)

    Step 1: Prepare Digital Negative

    Hybrid Digital/Analog Workflow:

    For contact printing with digital negatives, the process is identical to cyanotype/salt print with one critical difference:

    Gelatin silver papers have much higher contrast than alternative processes. Adjust your digital negative accordingly:

    1. Convert image to black & white
    2. Flatten contrast slightly compared to your original image
    3. Silver gelatin papers have ~7-10 stops dynamic range
    4. Aim for smooth, moderate contrast

    5. Avoid extreme blacks or blown highlights

    6. Invert the image (blacks become clear, whites become black)

    7. Mirror/flip horizontally
    8. Resize to A4 (210 x 297mm)
    9. Print on inkjet transparency film (same as cyanotype/salt print)

    Film and Printer:

    Use the same transparency film and printer setup as cyanotype: - Pictorico OHP Ultra Premium (professional choice) - Office supply transparencies (budget option) - Epson P700/P900 or similar pigment-based inkjet printer

    Alternative: Traditional Film Negatives:

    If you have access to medium/large format film cameras: - Shoot 6x7cm or 4x5" film negatives - Contact print directly to A4 paper (trimming to fit) - No digital negative required - traditional workflow

    Resource: Douglas Ethridge's book Silver Gelatin in the Digital Age provides comprehensive guidance on digital/analog hybrid workflows for contact printing.33

    Step 2: Set Up the Darkroom

    Lighting: - Work in complete darkness when handling unexposed paper - Use safelight (amber/red OC filter) positioned at least 1.2m (4 feet) from working surface - Test safelight safety: place unexposed paper under safelight for 5 minutes, develop - should show no fogging

    Chemistry Preparation:

    1. Developer: Mix according to manufacturer instructions
    2. Typical dilution: 1:9 (1 part developer, 9 parts water)

    3. Temperature: 20°C (68°F) - use thermometer to verify

    4. Stop Bath: 2% acetic acid solution

    5. Temperature: 18-24°C (65-75°F)

    6. Fixer: Mix according to instructions (typically 1:4 or 1:9)

    7. Temperature: 18-24°C (65-75°F)

    8. Washing Aid (optional but recommended): Dilute per instructions

    9. Reduces washing time from 60 minutes to 5-10 minutes

    Tray Setup: - Tray 1: Developer - Tray 2: Stop bath - Tray 3: Fixer - Tray 4: Water holding bath (until ready to wash)

    Step 3: Exposure

    1. Under safelight, place unexposed paper in contact frame (emulsion side up)
    2. Emulsion side is slightly glossy/shiny side

    3. Paper curls toward emulsion side

    4. Place digital negative on top (emulsion side DOWN, in contact with paper)

    5. Ensure tight contact - any gap reduces sharpness

    6. Close contact frame

    7. Expose to UV light:

    8. UV LED box: 10-30 seconds (test exposure required)
    9. Sunlight: 5-15 seconds (extremely fast compared to cyanotype/salt print)
    10. Dedicated UV exposure unit: Follow timer recommendations

    Exposure Testing:

    Unlike cyanotype and salt print, silver gelatin papers are extremely sensitive to UV light. Always make test strips first:

    1. Cover paper in contact frame with opaque card
    2. Expose in increments (e.g., 5, 10, 20, 40 seconds)
    3. Process test strip and evaluate results
    4. Choose optimal exposure time

    Step 4: Development

    Lighting: Work under safelight only

    1. Slide exposed paper into developer tray
    2. Emulsion side UP

    3. Gently agitate tray continuously

    4. Develop for recommended time (typically 1-3 minutes)

    5. Image will appear within 10-30 seconds
    6. Continue developing until full density achieved

    7. Do not "snatch" prints early - full development ensures archival stability

    8. Observe development under safelight:

    9. Highlights appear first
    10. Midtones and shadows gradually build density
    11. Stop when image looks slightly darker than desired (will lighten slightly in fixer)

    Step 5: Stop Bath

    1. Lift print from developer with tongs (use dedicated developer tongs)
    2. Drain briefly over developer tray (5-10 seconds)
    3. Immerse in stop bath for 30-60 seconds
    4. Agitate gently
    5. Stop bath neutralizes developer, halting development

    Step 6: Fixing

    1. Transfer print to fixer (use dedicated fixer tongs)
    2. Fix for recommended time (typically 5-10 minutes for fiber-based paper)
    3. Agitate occasionally

    4. Lights can be turned on after 1 minute in fixer (print is now stable)

    5. CRITICAL: Complete fixing is essential for archival permanence

    6. Incomplete fixing leads to yellowing and degradation over time
    7. Use two-bath fixing for maximum archival quality (fix in first bath 5 min, second bath 5 min)

    Step 7: Washing Aid (Optional but Recommended)

    1. Rinse print briefly in running water (1-2 minutes)
    2. Immerse in washing aid solution (Ilford Washaid, Kodak Hypo Clearing Agent) for 2-5 minutes
    3. Agitate occasionally
    4. Washing aid removes residual fixer much faster than water alone

    Why Use Washing Aid: - Reduces washing time from 60 minutes to 10-15 minutes - Conserves water (reduces water usage by ~60-70%) - Improves archival permanence

    Step 8: Washing

    Without Washing Aid: - Wash fiber-based prints for 60 minutes in running water - Change water frequently

    With Washing Aid: - Wash fiber-based prints for 10-15 minutes in running water - Archival print washer or tray siphon recommended

    Water Conservation:

    Traditional washing uses 2-5L per print. For small studios, consider:34

    • Recycled-water washer systems: Reuse rinse water for multiple prints
    • Tray siphon method: Continuous water flow through tray, more efficient than static soaking
    • Batch washing: Wash multiple prints together to share water usage

    Water Quality: - Final rinse with distilled or deionized water improves archival permanence (optional)

    Step 9: Drying

    1. Squeegee excess water from print surface
    2. Hang to air dry or lay flat on drying screens
    3. Fiber-based prints will curl during drying

    4. Air dry for 4-12 hours (depending on humidity)

    5. Flatten dried prints under weight (books, print flattener) for 24-48 hours

    Alternative Drying Methods: - Heated print dryer (glossy prints) - Blotter paper (absorbs water from fiber-based prints)

    On Starting Small:

    The most common mistake in alternative photography is over-investing before understanding the process. The recommended path:

    1. Start with cyanotype (low investment, immediate results, forgiving)
    2. Master contact printing fundamentals before adding complexity
    3. Expand to salt print once comfortable with coating and chemistry
    4. Add silver gelatin only when ready to commit to darkroom practice

    On Atmospheric Fine Art Imagery:

    All three processes excel at melancholic, atmospheric aesthetics:

    • Cyanotype: Moody blue tones, graphic silhouettes, coastal/marine subjects
    • Salt Print: Soft, nostalgic, vintage character, embedded image texture
    • Silver Gelatin: Classic fine art, long tonal range, smooth gradations

    The "window to another dimension" quality you seek comes from: - Thoughtful composition - Atmospheric subject matter (fog, mist, soft light) - Long tonal scales (salt print, silver gelatin) - Handmade process imperfections (lending authenticity and uniqueness)

    On Scaling to A3 and Beyond:

    Once comfortable with A4 contact printing:

    • A3 (12x16") requires larger contact frames, trays, and more chemistry/water
    • Costs scale proportionally (2x paper size = 2x chemistry/water)
    • UV exposure times remain similar (controlled by light intensity and distance, not size)
    • Hand-coating larger papers requires more practice for even coverage

    On Hand-Coated Emulsions and Custom Papers:

    After mastering commercial papers:

    • Liquid Light (silver gelatin emulsion) can be coated on any substrate (wood, fabric, glass)
    • DIY cyanotype sizing (gelatin, arrowroot) customizes tonal characteristics
    • Custom paper preparation (watercolor paper + sizing agents) creates unique surfaces

    This is the path from commercial production to experimental fine art practice.

    Appendix A: Glossary of Terms

    Archival Stability: The permanence of a photographic print when stored under proper conditions (pH-neutral materials, controlled humidity/temperature).

    Buffered Paper: Paper containing calcium carbonate to neutralize acids. Harmful to cyanotypes (fades blues over time). Use unbuffered papers for cyanotype.

    Contact Printing: Making prints the same size as the negative by placing them in direct contact during exposure (no enlargement).

    Dmax: Maximum density - the deepest black a print can achieve. Glossy papers have higher Dmax than matte papers.

    Emulsion: Light-sensitive coating on photographic paper or film (e.g., silver gelatin emulsion).

    Fiber-Based (FB) Paper: Traditional photographic paper with baryta layer and paper base (no resin coating). Requires longer washing, superior archival quality.

    Fixing: Chemical process that removes unexposed silver halides from photographic paper, stabilizing the image.

    Hypo: Common name for sodium thiosulfate (fixer).

    OHP Film: Overhead projector transparency film - used for printing digital negatives with inkjet printers.

    Prussian Blue: The deep blue pigment created in cyanotype process (ferric ferrocyanide).

    Resin-Coated (RC) Paper: Modern photographic paper with polyethylene coating on both sides. Fast processing, quick drying, less archival than fiber-based.

    Safelight: Darkroom light using specific wavelength/color that does not expose photographic paper (amber/red for silver gelatin, yellow for salt print).

    Sensitizer: Light-sensitive chemical coating applied to paper (e.g., silver nitrate for salt print, ferric ammonium citrate + potassium ferricyanide for cyanotype).

    Toning: Chemical process that alters image color and/or improves archival stability (gold toner, selenium toner, sepia toner).

    UV Sensitivity: Photographic materials sensitive to ultraviolet light (cyanotype, salt print) rather than visible light (silver gelatin).

    Variable Contrast Paper: Photographic paper whose contrast can be controlled by filtration or LED head color (e.g., Ilford Multigrade). Eliminates need for multiple graded papers.

    Washing Aid: Chemical solution (e.g., Ilford Washaid, Kodak Hypo Clearing Agent) that removes residual fixer faster than water alone, reducing washing time and water usage.

    Appendix B: Troubleshooting Guide

    Cyanotype Issues:

    Problem Cause Solution
    Pale blue after washing Underexposure Increase exposure time
    Uneven blue density Streaky coating Use glass coating rod, practice even application
    Yellow stains on print Insufficient washing Wash longer, change water more frequently
    Blue fades over time Stored in buffered materials Use unbuffered storage materials

    Salt Print Issues:

    Problem Cause Solution
    Weak image, low density Underexposure or weak sensitizer Increase exposure time, use 12% silver nitrate solution
    Mottled, uneven tones Uneven sensitizing Coat more evenly, use glass rod
    Print fades quickly Not toned Gold or selenium tone all prints for archival stability
    Purple-brown vs warm brown Toning chemistry Gold toner = warm tones, selenium = cooler tones

    Silver Gelatin Issues:

    Problem Cause Solution
    Fogged (overall gray) print Safelight too bright or too close Test safelight safety, increase distance or use dimmer bulb
    Weak blacks, low contrast Underdevelopment or underexposure Develop for full recommended time, increase exposure
    Yellow stains after drying Insufficient fixing or washing Use two-bath fixer, wash longer, use washing aid
    Curled fiber-based prints Normal for FB paper Flatten under weight for 24-48 hours after drying
