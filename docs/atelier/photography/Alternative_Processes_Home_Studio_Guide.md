## Part 1: Cyanotype

### 1.1 Overview

**What is Cyanotype?**

Cyanotype is a photographic printing process that produces cyan-blue prints. Invented by Sir John Herschel in 1842, it uses iron salts sensitive to UV light to create the distinctive Prussian blue image.[^3] Popularized by Anna Atkins in her 1843 botanical documentation *Photographs of British Algae*, it remains the most accessible alternative process for beginners.[^4]

[^3]: [Historic New Orleans Collection - Cyanotype Process: 1842–today](https://hnoc.org/virtual-exhibitions/from_daguerreotype_to_digital/cyanotype-process)
[^4]: [Natural History Museum - Anna Atkins's cyanotypes: the first book of photographs](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/anna-atkins-cyanotypes-the-first-book-of-photographs.html)

**Visual Character:**
- Vibrant Prussian blue color
- Sharp detail with visible paper texture
- High clarity, somewhat high contrast (~grade 3)
- Tendency to block up shadow detail
- Best suited for: botanical subjects, landscapes with open skies, graphic compositions

**Difficulty:** Beginner-friendly  
**Toxicity:** Low (ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide are irritants, not highly toxic)  
**Water Usage:** Low (~500ml per A4 print for washing)  
**Archival Stability:** Extremely archival when stored in unbuffered, pH-neutral materials[^5]

[^5]: [Preservation Self-Assessment Program - Photographic Prints](https://psap.library.illinois.edu/collection-id-guide/photoprint)

### 1.5 Contact Printing Workflow (A4 Size)

#### Step 1: Prepare Digital Negative

**Software:**
- Adobe Photoshop, GIMP, or Affinity Photo
- Convert image to black & white
- Adjust contrast (cyanotype has ~3 stops dynamic range - increase contrast slightly)
- **Invert** the image (black becomes clear, white becomes black)
- **Mirror/flip** horizontally (image will reverse during printing)
- Resize to A4 (210 x 297mm)

**Printing the Negative:**

For A4 home printing, use:[^11]

- **Film:** Inkjet transparency film (OHP film)
  - [Cyanoprints.com A4 Inkjet Transparencies](https://www.cyanoprints.com/products/transparencies-for-inkjet-printers) (50 sheets, designed for cyanotype)[^12]
  - Office Depot/Office supply generic transparencies work adequately
  - Pictorico OHP Ultra Premium (professional choice, higher density)

- **Printer:** Inkjet printer with **pigment-based black ink** (NOT dye-based)
  - Epson EcoTank series (budget option)
  - Epson P700/P900 (professional option, see note below)
  - Canon PIXMA Pro series

**Epson P800/P900 "Pizza Wheel" Issue:**

If using Epson P800 or P900 printers, be aware of the "pizza wheel" marking problem:[^13]

- Default rear feed causes roller marks on transparencies
- **Solutions:**
  - Use **front loading** mode (disengages pizza rollers) + tape transparency to poster board[^14]
  - Increase drying time from 0 to 10 in printer settings (slower but minimizes marks)
  - European firmware update enables "no eject roller" option
  - Use QuadToneRIP software for better ink control

[^11]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Digital negatives](https://www.alternativephotography.com/digital-negatives/)
[^12]: [Cyanoprints.com - Inkjet Transparencies for Cyanotype Negatives](https://www.cyanoprints.com/products/transparencies-for-inkjet-printers)
[^13]: [Photrio.com - Pt/Pd digital negative platen gap setting for Epson P900](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/pt-pd-digital-negative-platen-gap-setting-for-epson-p900-pizza-wheel-issue-on-pictorico.205202/)
[^14]: [Photrio.com - Digital negs for pt/pd on Epson P900 challenge](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/digital-negs-for-pt-pd-on-epson-p900-challenge.202705/)

**Printer Settings:**
- Media type: "OHP Transparency" or "Premium Matte Presentation Paper"
- Print quality: Highest available
- Color management: None (print as grayscale)

#### Step 2: Coat the Paper

**Lighting:** Work under normal room light (cyanotype chemistry is not light-sensitive until mixed)

1. Mix equal parts Solution A (ferric ammonium citrate) and Solution B (potassium ferricyanide)
   - Mix only what you need - combined solution has ~24 hour working life
   - For A4 size: ~8-10ml total (4-5ml each solution)

2. Coat the paper evenly:
   - Use foam brush or glass rod
   - Coat in dim light (not critical, but helps see coverage)
   - Brush/spread evenly across paper surface
   - Aim for smooth, streak-free coating
   - **Allow to dry completely in darkness** (15-30 minutes in dry climate, longer in humid conditions)

**Tips:**
- Coated paper is now light-sensitive - store in darkness until exposure
- Coated paper can be stored for weeks in a dark, dry environment
- Coating under low humidity produces better results

#### Step 3: Exposure

1. Place dried, coated paper in contact frame (coated side up)
2. Place digital negative on top (emulsion side DOWN, touching the coated paper)
3. Close frame or add glass + weight to ensure tight contact
4. Expose to UV light:
   - **Sunlight:** 15-30 minutes (watch for color shift from yellow-green to gray-blue)
   - **UV LED box:** 5-10 minutes (test your specific setup)
   - Proper exposure shows visible image with dark blue-gray highlights before washing

**Judging Exposure:**
- Underexposed: Pale blue after washing, weak image
- Properly exposed: Deep Prussian blue, strong image density
- Overexposed: Very dark, blocked shadows (less common problem)

#### Step 4: Washing and Finishing

1. **First rinse:** Gently rinse print under running water (cold tap water is fine)
   - Image will appear muddy yellow-green at first
   - Continue rinsing until water runs clear
   - Blue color will deepen dramatically during washing

2. **Washing tray:** Soak print in tray of clean water for 5-10 minutes
   - Change water 2-3 times
   - Total water usage: ~500ml per print

3. **Optional oxidation bath:** Soak in dilute hydrogen peroxide (3%, available at pharmacy) for 1-2 minutes
   - Deepens blue color and speeds oxidation
   - Not required - air oxidation will achieve same result in 24 hours

4. **Drying:** Hang or lay flat to dry
   - Blue will continue to deepen over 24-48 hours as oxidation completes
   - Avoid direct sunlight during drying (though cyanotypes are light-stable once dry)

## Part 2: Salt Print (Salted Paper Print)

### 2.1 Overview

**What is Salt Print?**

Salt printing, invented by William Henry Fox Talbot in the mid-1830s, was the dominant paper-based photographic process from 1839 to approximately 1860.[^16] The process involves two steps: first salting paper with sodium chloride (table salt), then sensitizing with silver nitrate to create a UV-sensitive surface.

[^16]: [Wikipedia - Salt print](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_print)

**Visual Character:**
- Warm brown to purple-brown tones (depending on toning)
- Soft, matte appearance
- Image embedded in paper fibers (not on surface layer)
- Long tonal scale with delicate highlights and smooth shadows
- Extremely low contrast compared to cyanotype[^17]
- Best suited for: portraits, soft landscapes, vintage aesthetic, fine art prints

[^17]: [Koraks Tinkers - Opposites attract: Salted paper and cyanotype curves](https://tinker.koraks.nl/photography/opposites-attract-salted-paper-and-cyanotype-curves/)

**Difficulty:** Intermediate (requires two-step coating, careful silver nitrate handling)  
**Toxicity:** Moderate (silver nitrate is corrosive and stains skin)  
**Water Usage:** Moderate (~1L per A4 print)  
**Archival Stability:** Requires gold or selenium toning for long-term stability[^18]

[^18]: [Artspace - From C-Print to Silver Gelatin: The Ultimate Guide to Photo Prints](http://www.artspace.com/magazine/art_101/whats-a-chromogenic-pigment-or-gelatin-print-the-ultimate-guide-to-digital-and-chemical-photo)

### 2.5 Contact Printing Workflow (A4 Size)

#### Step 1: Prepare Digital Negative

**Same process as cyanotype** (see Section 1.5, Step 1), with one important difference:

- Salt prints have a **long tonal scale** and **low contrast**[^23]
- DO NOT increase contrast aggressively like cyanotype
- Adjust for slightly higher contrast than the original image, but much less than cyanotype
- Test exposure and adjust digital negative contrast accordingly

[^23]: [Koraks Tinkers - Opposites attract: Salted paper and cyanotype curves](https://tinker.koraks.nl/photography/opposites-attract-salted-paper-and-cyanotype-curves/)

Use the same transparency film and printer workflow as cyanotype.

#### Step 2: Salt the Paper

**Lighting:** Work under normal room light (salting step is not light-sensitive)

1. Prepare 2% salt solution:
   - 20g non-iodized table salt
   - 1 liter distilled water
   - Mix thoroughly until dissolved

2. Pour solution into shallow tray (A4 size or larger)

3. Float or soak paper in salt solution for 2-3 minutes
   - Ensure even coverage
   - Flip paper if needed

4. Hang paper to dry completely
   - Drying time: 30-60 minutes depending on humidity
   - **Salted paper can be stored indefinitely** in dry conditions

**Note:** Some practitioners add gelatin or arrowroot starch to the salting solution for improved surface quality and tonal characteristics. This is optional for beginners.

#### Step 3: Sensitize the Paper

**Lighting:** Work under **safelight** (7W yellow/red bulb at 1 meter distance) or dim tungsten light[^24]

[^24]: [Lomography - Alternative Processes: How to Make Salt Prints](https://www.lomography.com/magazine/234914-alternative-processes-how-to-make-salt-prints)

**CRITICAL SAFETY:** Wear thick rubber gloves and eye protection. Silver nitrate is corrosive and stains skin black permanently.

1. Prepare silver nitrate sensitizer:
   - 10-12% solution is standard (100-120g silver nitrate per liter distilled water)
   - Start with 12% for denser negatives, 10% for thinner negatives

2. Coat the dried salted paper with silver nitrate solution:
   - Use foam brush or glass rod
   - Coat evenly in dim safelight conditions
   - Work relatively quickly - paper is now light-sensitive

3. Dry the sensitized paper in complete darkness
   - Drying time: 15-30 minutes
   - **Use immediately after drying** - sensitized paper degrades within hours

**Tips:**
- Sensitize paper shortly before exposure (same day)
- Store sensitized paper in dark, dry environment if not using immediately
- Paper will have a pale yellow-green color when sensitized

#### Step 4: Exposure

Same process as cyanotype:

1. Place dried, sensitized paper in contact frame (sensitized side up)
2. Place digital negative on top (emulsion side down, in contact)
3. Close frame to ensure tight contact
4. Expose to UV light:
   - **Sunlight:** 15-60 minutes (longer than cyanotype - salt prints print more slowly)
   - **UV LED box:** 10-20 minutes (test your specific setup)
   - Image should be clearly visible with dark reddish-brown color before processing

**Judging Exposure:**
- Properly exposed: Visible image with warm reddish-brown color, good shadow detail
- Underexposed: Pale, weak image after fixing
- Overexposed: Very dark, muddy image (less common - salt prints are forgiving)

#### Step 5: Fixing

**Lighting:** Work under safelight or dim room light

1. Prepare fixer bath:
   - Sodium thiosulfate (hypo): 20% solution (200g per liter distilled water)
   - Temperature: Room temperature (20°C/68°F)

2. Immerse print in fixer for 5-10 minutes
   - Image will lighten and stabilize
   - Gently agitate tray occasionally

3. **CRITICAL:** Fix until all unexposed silver is removed
   - Incomplete fixing leads to print degradation over time

#### Step 6: Washing

1. Rinse print under running water for 2-3 minutes

2. Wash in tray of fresh water for 20-30 minutes
   - Change water 3-4 times
   - Total water usage: ~1L per A4 print

3. Final rinse under running water for 2-3 minutes

**Water Quality:**
- Distilled or deionized water is ideal for final washes
- Tap water is acceptable if not heavily chlorinated or hard

#### Step 7: Toning (Essential for Archival Stability)

**WHY TONING IS CRITICAL:**

Untoned salt prints are sensitive to light and will fade over time. Gold or selenium toning converts the silver image to a more stable compound, ensuring archival longevity.[^25]

[^25]: [Artspace - From C-Print to Silver Gelatin: The Ultimate Guide to Photo Prints](http://www.artspace.com/magazine/art_101/whats-a-chromogenic-pigment-or-gelatin-print-the-ultimate-guide-to-digital-and-chemical-photo)

**Gold Toning (Recommended):**

1. Prepare gold toner bath:
   - Sodium gold chloride: 1% solution
   - Borax: Optional addition for warmer tones
   - Dilute as per manufacturer instructions (varies by product)

2. Immerse washed print in gold toner for 5-10 minutes
   - Image will shift to warmer purple-brown tones
   - Increases archival stability significantly

3. Wash again for 10-15 minutes in fresh water

**Selenium Toning (Alternative):**

- Produces cooler brown tones
- Also provides archival protection
- Follow manufacturer dilution instructions (typically 1:9 to 1:20)

#### Step 8: Final Drying

Hang or lay flat to dry completely. Salt prints are fully processed and archival (if toned properly) once dry.

## Part 3: Gelatin Silver Contact Printing

### 3.1 Overview

**What is Gelatin Silver Contact Printing?**

Gelatin silver printing is the traditional black-and-white darkroom process, developed in the 1870s-1880s and refined throughout the 20th century. It uses light-sensitive silver halides embedded in a gelatin emulsion coated on paper. Contact printing refers to making prints the same size as the negative by placing them in direct contact during exposure (rather than enlarging in a darkroom enlarger).[^28]

[^28]: [The Darkroom Photo Lab - A Guide to Gelatin Silver Prints](https://thedarkroom.com/a-guide-to-gelatin-silver-prints/)

**Visual Character:**
- True neutral black tones (no color cast)
- Maximum sharpness and detail resolution
- Longest tonal range of all analog processes
- Surface can be glossy, semi-gloss, or matte depending on paper
- Deep blacks, brilliant whites, smooth gradations
- Best suited for: Fine art photography, classic black & white imagery, maximum image quality

**Difficulty:** Intermediate (requires full darkroom or changing bag + chemistry knowledge)  
**Toxicity:** Moderate to High (developer contains hydroquinone/metol, fixer contains acetic acid)  
**Water Usage:** High (~2-5L per A4 print, depending on washer efficiency)  
**Archival Stability:** Highest archival stability when properly processed and washed[^29]

[^29]: [Preservation Self-Assessment Program - Photographic Prints](https://psap.library.illinois.edu/collection-id-guide/photoprint)

### 3.5 Contact Printing Workflow (A4 Size)

#### Step 1: Prepare Digital Negative

**Hybrid Digital/Analog Workflow:**

For contact printing with digital negatives, the process is identical to cyanotype/salt print with one critical difference:

**Gelatin silver papers have much higher contrast** than alternative processes. Adjust your digital negative accordingly:

1. Convert image to black & white
2. **Flatten contrast slightly** compared to your original image
   - Silver gelatin papers have ~7-10 stops dynamic range
   - Aim for smooth, moderate contrast
   - Avoid extreme blacks or blown highlights

3. Invert the image (blacks become clear, whites become black)
4. Mirror/flip horizontally
5. Resize to A4 (210 x 297mm)
6. Print on inkjet transparency film (same as cyanotype/salt print)

**Film and Printer:**

Use the same transparency film and printer setup as cyanotype:
- Pictorico OHP Ultra Premium (professional choice)
- Office supply transparencies (budget option)
- Epson P700/P900 or similar pigment-based inkjet printer

**Alternative: Traditional Film Negatives:**

If you have access to medium/large format film cameras:
- Shoot 6x7cm or 4x5" film negatives
- Contact print directly to A4 paper (trimming to fit)
- No digital negative required - traditional workflow

**Resource:** Douglas Ethridge's book *Silver Gelatin in the Digital Age* provides comprehensive guidance on digital/analog hybrid workflows for contact printing.[^33]

[^33]: [Amazon - Silver Gelatin in the Digital Age](https://www.amazon.com/Silver-Gelatin-Digital-Step-Step/dp/1032381779)

#### Step 2: Set Up the Darkroom

**Lighting:**
- Work in **complete darkness** when handling unexposed paper
- Use **safelight** (amber/red OC filter) positioned at least 1.2m (4 feet) from working surface
- Test safelight safety: place unexposed paper under safelight for 5 minutes, develop - should show no fogging

**Chemistry Preparation:**

1. **Developer:** Mix according to manufacturer instructions
   - Typical dilution: 1:9 (1 part developer, 9 parts water)
   - Temperature: 20°C (68°F) - use thermometer to verify

2. **Stop Bath:** 2% acetic acid solution
   - Temperature: 18-24°C (65-75°F)

3. **Fixer:** Mix according to instructions (typically 1:4 or 1:9)
   - Temperature: 18-24°C (65-75°F)

4. **Washing Aid (optional but recommended):** Dilute per instructions
   - Reduces washing time from 60 minutes to 5-10 minutes

**Tray Setup:**
- Tray 1: Developer
- Tray 2: Stop bath
- Tray 3: Fixer
- Tray 4: Water holding bath (until ready to wash)

#### Step 3: Exposure

1. Under safelight, place unexposed paper in contact frame (emulsion side up)
   - Emulsion side is slightly glossy/shiny side
   - Paper curls toward emulsion side

2. Place digital negative on top (emulsion side DOWN, in contact with paper)
   - Ensure tight contact - any gap reduces sharpness

3. Close contact frame

4. Expose to UV light:
   - **UV LED box:** 10-30 seconds (test exposure required)
   - **Sunlight:** 5-15 seconds (extremely fast compared to cyanotype/salt print)
   - **Dedicated UV exposure unit:** Follow timer recommendations

**Exposure Testing:**

Unlike cyanotype and salt print, silver gelatin papers are **extremely sensitive** to UV light. Always make test strips first:

1. Cover paper in contact frame with opaque card
2. Expose in increments (e.g., 5, 10, 20, 40 seconds)
3. Process test strip and evaluate results
4. Choose optimal exposure time

#### Step 4: Development

**Lighting:** Work under safelight only

1. Slide exposed paper into developer tray
   - Emulsion side UP
   - Gently agitate tray continuously

2. Develop for recommended time (typically 1-3 minutes)
   - Image will appear within 10-30 seconds
   - Continue developing until full density achieved
   - Do not "snatch" prints early - full development ensures archival stability

3. Observe development under safelight:
   - Highlights appear first
   - Midtones and shadows gradually build density
   - Stop when image looks slightly darker than desired (will lighten slightly in fixer)

#### Step 5: Stop Bath

1. Lift print from developer with tongs (use dedicated developer tongs)
2. Drain briefly over developer tray (5-10 seconds)
3. Immerse in stop bath for 30-60 seconds
   - Agitate gently
   - Stop bath neutralizes developer, halting development

#### Step 6: Fixing

1. Transfer print to fixer (use dedicated fixer tongs)
2. Fix for recommended time (typically 5-10 minutes for fiber-based paper)
   - Agitate occasionally
   - Lights can be turned on after 1 minute in fixer (print is now stable)

3. **CRITICAL:** Complete fixing is essential for archival permanence
   - Incomplete fixing leads to yellowing and degradation over time
   - Use two-bath fixing for maximum archival quality (fix in first bath 5 min, second bath 5 min)

#### Step 7: Washing Aid (Optional but Recommended)

1. Rinse print briefly in running water (1-2 minutes)
2. Immerse in washing aid solution (Ilford Washaid, Kodak Hypo Clearing Agent) for 2-5 minutes
   - Agitate occasionally
   - Washing aid removes residual fixer much faster than water alone

**Why Use Washing Aid:**
- Reduces washing time from 60 minutes to 10-15 minutes
- Conserves water (reduces water usage by ~60-70%)
- Improves archival permanence

#### Step 8: Washing

**Without Washing Aid:**
- Wash fiber-based prints for 60 minutes in running water
- Change water frequently

**With Washing Aid:**
- Wash fiber-based prints for 10-15 minutes in running water
- Archival print washer or tray siphon recommended

**Water Conservation:**

Traditional washing uses 2-5L per print. For small studios, consider:[^34]

- **Recycled-water washer systems:** Reuse rinse water for multiple prints
- **Tray siphon method:** Continuous water flow through tray, more efficient than static soaking
- **Batch washing:** Wash multiple prints together to share water usage

[^34]: [Silver and Gelatin - Reduced water usage print washer](https://justinrichards.xyz/blog/reduced-water-usage-print-washer/)

**Water Quality:**
- Final rinse with distilled or deionized water improves archival permanence (optional)

#### Step 9: Drying

1. Squeegee excess water from print surface
2. Hang to air dry or lay flat on drying screens
   - Fiber-based prints will curl during drying
   - Air dry for 4-12 hours (depending on humidity)

3. Flatten dried prints under weight (books, print flattener) for 24-48 hours

**Alternative Drying Methods:**
- Heated print dryer (glossy prints)
- Blotter paper (absorbs water from fiber-based prints)

### 4.3 Process Complexity Ladder

**Easiest to Hardest:**

1. **Cyanotype**
   - No darkroom required
   - Forgiving exposure times
   - Simple one-step coating
   - Safe chemistry
   - Minimal water usage

2. **Salt Print**
   - No darkroom (safelight only)
   - Two-step coating (salting + sensitizing)
   - Requires silver nitrate handling (corrosive)
   - Toning required for archival prints
   - Moderate water usage

3. **Silver Gelatin Contact Printing**
   - Full darkroom or changing bag required
   - Multiple chemistry steps (developer, stop, fixer)
   - Precise timing and temperature control
   - High water usage for washing
   - More complex troubleshooting

**Recommended Learning Path:**

1. Start with **cyanotype** to learn contact printing fundamentals
2. Progress to **salt print** to learn multi-step coating and silver chemistry handling
3. Move to **silver gelatin** once comfortable with darkroom workflow

- **Silver Gelatin:** Classic fine art photography, architectural details, high-detail landscapes

This approach allows you to:
- Reach different market segments
- Showcase technical versatility
- Command varied price points
- Tell different visual stories

### 4.5 Space and Setup Requirements

**Minimal Space (10-20 sq ft):**
- **Process:** Cyanotype only
- **Setup:** Small table for coating, trays for washing, UV exposure area (can be outdoor)
- **Storage:** Dry chemistry storage, paper storage, negative storage

**Small Studio (50-100 sq ft):**
- **Process:** Cyanotype + Salt Print
- **Setup:** Coating table with safelight, washing station, UV exposure area
- **Storage:** Chemistry (amber bottles), papers, negative files

**Full Darkroom (100-200+ sq ft):**
- **Process:** All three (Cyanotype, Salt Print, Silver Gelatin)
- **Setup:** Light-tight room, safelight, wet side (trays/sink), dry side (coating/exposure), print washer
- **Storage:** Chemistry storage, paper safe, negative files, drying racks

**Recommendations for Small Apartment:**

Start with **cyanotype** (requires minimal space, no darkroom):
- Use bathroom or kitchen for washing
- Coat/expose in any room with table and window access
- Store chemistry and papers in closet

Add **salt print** once comfortable:
- Same space as cyanotype (safelight in bathroom/kitchen for sensitizing step)

**Silver gelatin requires dedicated darkroom** - not ideal for very small apartments unless using changing bag method (more cumbersome).

**On Starting Small:**

1. Start with cyanotype (low investment, immediate results, forgiving)
2. Master contact printing fundamentals before adding complexity
3. Expand to salt print once comfortable with coating and chemistry
4. Add silver gelatin only when ready to commit to darkroom practice

**On Atmospheric Fine Art Imagery:**

All three processes excel at **melancholic, atmospheric aesthetics**:

- **Cyanotype:** Moody blue tones, graphic silhouettes, coastal/marine subjects
- **Salt Print:** Soft, nostalgic, vintage character, embedded image texture
- **Silver Gelatin:** Classic fine art, long tonal range, smooth gradations

The "window to another dimension" quality you seek comes from:
- Thoughtful composition
- Atmospheric subject matter (fog, mist, soft light)
- Long tonal scales (salt print, silver gelatin)

**On Scaling to A3 and Beyond:**

Once comfortable with A4 contact printing:

- A3 (12x16") requires larger contact frames, trays, and more chemistry/water
- Costs scale proportionally (2x paper size = 2x chemistry/water)
- UV exposure times remain similar (controlled by light intensity and distance, not size)
- Hand-coating larger papers requires more practice for even coverage

**On Hand-Coated Emulsions and Custom Papers:**

After mastering commercial papers:

- **Liquid Light** (silver gelatin emulsion) can be coated on any substrate (wood, fabric, glass)
- **DIY cyanotype sizing** (gelatin, arrowroot) customizes tonal characteristics
- **Custom paper preparation** (watercolor paper + sizing agents) creates unique surfaces

This is the path from **commercial production** to **experimental fine art practice**.

## Appendices

### Appendix A: Glossary of Terms

**Archival Stability:** The permanence of a photographic print when stored under proper conditions (pH-neutral materials, controlled humidity/temperature).

**Buffered Paper:** Paper containing calcium carbonate to neutralize acids. Harmful to cyanotypes (fades blues over time). Use unbuffered papers for cyanotype.

**Contact Printing:** Making prints the same size as the negative by placing them in direct contact during exposure (no enlargement).

**Dmax:** Maximum density - the deepest black a print can achieve. Glossy papers have higher Dmax than matte papers.

**Emulsion:** Light-sensitive coating on photographic paper or film (e.g., silver gelatin emulsion).

**Fiber-Based (FB) Paper:** Traditional photographic paper with baryta layer and paper base (no resin coating). Requires longer washing, superior archival quality.

**Fixing:** Chemical process that removes unexposed silver halides from photographic paper, stabilizing the image.

**Hypo:** Common name for sodium thiosulfate (fixer).

**OHP Film:** Overhead projector transparency film - used for printing digital negatives with inkjet printers.

**Prussian Blue:** The deep blue pigment created in cyanotype process (ferric ferrocyanide).

**Resin-Coated (RC) Paper:** Modern photographic paper with polyethylene coating on both sides. Fast processing, quick drying, less archival than fiber-based.

**Safelight:** Darkroom light using specific wavelength/color that does not expose photographic paper (amber/red for silver gelatin, yellow for salt print).

**Sensitizer:** Light-sensitive chemical coating applied to paper (e.g., silver nitrate for salt print, ferric ammonium citrate + potassium ferricyanide for cyanotype).

**Toning:** Chemical process that alters image color and/or improves archival stability (gold toner, selenium toner, sepia toner).

**UV Sensitivity:** Photographic materials sensitive to ultraviolet light (cyanotype, salt print) rather than visible light (silver gelatin).

**Variable Contrast Paper:** Photographic paper whose contrast can be controlled by filtration or LED head color (e.g., Ilford Multigrade). Eliminates need for multiple graded papers.

**Washing Aid:** Chemical solution (e.g., Ilford Washaid, Kodak Hypo Clearing Agent) that removes residual fixer faster than water alone, reducing washing time and water usage.

### Appendix B: Troubleshooting Guide

**Cyanotype Issues:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Pale blue after washing | Underexposure | Increase exposure time |
| Uneven blue density | Streaky coating | Use glass coating rod, practice even application |
| Yellow stains on print | Insufficient washing | Wash longer, change water more frequently |
| Blue fades over time | Stored in buffered materials | Use unbuffered storage materials |

**Salt Print Issues:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Weak image, low density | Underexposure or weak sensitizer | Increase exposure time, use 12% silver nitrate solution |
| Mottled, uneven tones | Uneven sensitizing | Coat more evenly, use glass rod |
| Print fades quickly | Not toned | Gold or selenium tone all prints for archival stability |
| Purple-brown vs warm brown | Toning chemistry | Gold toner = warm tones, selenium = cooler tones |

**Silver Gelatin Issues:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Fogged (overall gray) print | Safelight too bright or too close | Test safelight safety, increase distance or use dimmer bulb |
| Weak blacks, low contrast | Underdevelopment or underexposure | Develop for full recommended time, increase exposure |
| Yellow stains after drying | Insufficient fixing or washing | Use two-bath fixer, wash longer, use washing aid |
| Curled fiber-based prints | Normal for FB paper | Flatten under weight for 24-48 hours after drying |
