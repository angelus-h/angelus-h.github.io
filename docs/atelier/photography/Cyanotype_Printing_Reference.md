# Cyanotype Printing Reference

**A Technical Field Guide for Serious Photographers and Fine Art Printers**

*Last Updated: 2026-05-25*

## Introduction

This guide provides technically grounded information about cyanotype printing for serious analog photographers, fine art printers, and alternative process practitioners. All chemistry behavior, archival properties, and technical specifications are verified against manufacturer documentation, conservation research, and experienced printmaker workflows.

Where information is uncertain or debated among practitioners, these points are explicitly noted with multiple perspectives presented.

## 1. What Cyanotype Actually Is

### Historical Context

The cyanotype process was invented in **1842 by Sir John F. W. Herschel** (1792–1871), an astronomer and scientist who intended it for reproducing mathematical tables and technical drawings.[^1] The process uses paper coated with iron salts that, after sun exposure and water washing, creates a white image on a deep blue background—the original "blueprint."

[^1]: [Historic New Orleans Collection - Cyanotype Process: 1842–today](https://hnoc.org/virtual-exhibitions/from_daguerreotype_to_digital/cyanotype-process)

### Anna Atkins and Botanical Documentation

**Anna Atkins** transformed cyanotype from technical reproduction into an artistic medium. Through her father and husband, she knew both William Henry Fox Talbot (pioneer of silver-based photography) and Sir John Herschel (inventor of the cyanotype).[^2] In 1843, she self-published *Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions*, making every print herself—the first book ever illustrated entirely with photographic images.[^2]

Only 20 known copies exist today, 15 of which are substantially complete, housed in major museums including the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Victoria and Albert Museum.[^3][^4]

[^2]: [Natural History Museum - Anna Atkins's cyanotypes: the first book of photographs](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/anna-atkins-cyanotypes-the-first-book-of-photographs.html)
[^3]: [The Metropolitan Museum of Art - Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/286656)
[^4]: [V&A Museum - A blueprint for the future: Cyanotypes by Anna Atkins](https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/caring-for-our-collections/a-blueprint-for-the-future-cyanotypes-by-anna-atkins)

### Chemical Process Fundamentals

Cyanotype relies on the photoreduction of ferric (iron III) compounds to ferrous (iron II) compounds under ultraviolet light. The ferrous ions then react with ferricyanide to form Prussian blue (ferric ferrocyanide), an intensely colored, stable pigment.

**Chemical Formula:**
- Ferric ammonium citrate: (NH₄)₅Fe(C₆H₄O₇)₂
- Potassium ferricyanide: K₃[Fe(CN)₆]
- Prussian blue: Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃[^5]

[^5]: [American Chemical Society - Potassium ferricyanide](https://www.acs.org/molecule-of-the-week/archive/p/potassium-ferricyanide.html)

### UV Sensitivity and Tonal Behavior

Cyanotype is sensitive only to ultraviolet and blue-violet light (300-450nm wavelength), making it safe to work under yellow/red safelighting. This limited spectral sensitivity gives cyanotype its characteristic tonal rendering:

- **Strengths:** Long tonal scale, excellent shadow detail retention, contact printing simplicity
- **Limitations:** Density range of ~1.0-1.2 log (traditional formula), requiring high-contrast negatives; inherently blue monochrome (unless toned)

### Artistic Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths:**
- Non-toxic, beginner-friendly chemistry
- No darkroom required
- Archival stability when properly processed
- Unique atmospheric blue tone suited to landscapes, botanicals, architectural subjects
- Relatively inexpensive materials

**Weaknesses:**
- Limited native contrast range (traditional formula)
- Blue color can feel cliché if subject selection is not thoughtful
- Requires contact-printing workflow (limits print size to negative size)
- Humidity-sensitive during coating and exposure
- Alkaline-sensitive (degrades in alkaline environments)

## 2. Cyanotype Chemistry

### Traditional Cyanotype Formula

The traditional cyanotype uses two stock solutions mixed just before coating:[^6]

**Solution A (Ferric Ammonium Citrate):**
- 25 g ferric ammonium citrate (green type)
- Distilled water to make 100 ml total volume

**Solution B (Potassium Ferricyanide):**
- 10 g potassium ferricyanide
- Distilled water to make 100 ml total volume

**Working Solution:**
- Mix equal parts Solution A and Solution B immediately before use
- Alternative proportions: 8.1% (w/v) potassium ferricyanide + 20% ferric ammonium citrate

[^6]: [Mike Ware - The Traditional Cyanotype Process](https://www.mikeware.co.uk/mikeware/Traditional_Cyanotype.html)

### New Cyanotype Formula (Mike Ware)

Mike Ware developed an improved formula that replaces ferric ammonium citrate with **ammonium iron(III) oxalate**, offering:[^7]

- **2-4× faster exposure** (2-4 minutes vs. 20-30 minutes under UV lamps)
- **Greater density range** (~2.4 log vs. 1.0-1.2 log for traditional)
- **Deeper blue intensity**
- **Better negative contact** (sensitizer absorbs into paper, not tacky)

**Trade-offs:**
- More toxic if ingested
- Slightly more complex preparation
- Requires more experience in chemical manipulation

[^7]: [Mike Ware - The New Cyanotype Process](https://www.mikeware.co.uk/mikeware/New_Cyanotype_Process.html)

### Solution Preparation

**Critical requirements:**
- Use distilled or deionized water (tap water minerals can contaminate)
- Dissolve chemicals at room temperature (68°F / 20°C)
- Stir gently until fully dissolved
- Filter through fine filter or coffee filter to remove particles[^8]

[^8]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Cyanotype – the classic process](https://www.alternativephotography.com/cyanotype-classic-process/)

### Storage Stability

| Solution | Storage Condition | Shelf Life | Notes |
|----------|-------------------|------------|-------|
| **Solution A (unmixed)** | Dark glass/opaque plastic, sealed | Indefinite | Prone to mold growth over time |
| **Solution B (unmixed)** | Dark glass/opaque plastic, sealed | Indefinite | Very stable |
| **Mixed working solution** | Amber glass bottle, refrigerated | 2-3 weeks | Decomposes progressively |
| **Coated paper (unexposed)** | Dark, sealed bag with desiccant | Several months | Humidity-sensitive |

**Reality vs. Manufacturer Claims:**
While manufacturers claim mixed solutions last 2-3 weeks, real-world experience shows degradation can begin after 1 week, especially if exposed to light or warmth. Pre-coat paper fresh before critical work.[^9]

[^9]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Papers for traditional / classic cyanotype process](https://www.alternativephotography.com/papers-for-traditional-classic-cyanotype-process-1613-research-for-free/)

### Contamination Risks

**Common contaminants:**
- **Iron contamination** from tap water → uneven blue staining
- **Alkaline chemicals** (soap, detergent residue on brushes) → bleached highlights
- **Dust particles** → white specks in coating
- **Mold growth** in ferric ammonium citrate solution → irregular staining

**Prevention:**
- Use only distilled/deionized water
- Dedicated brushes never used with alkaline materials
- Filter solutions before use
- Clean work surface with distilled water before coating

### Humidity Sensitivity

Relative humidity significantly affects cyanotype behavior at all stages:[^10]

| Humidity Level | Coating Effect | Exposure Effect | Drying Effect |
|----------------|----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **<40% (Low)** | Difficult absorption, uneven coating | 1.5-2× longer exposure required | Fast drying, grainy texture |
| **50-60% (Ideal)** | Smooth coating, even absorption | Normal exposure time | Moderate drying, smooth tones |
| **>70% (High)** | Premature blueing, tacky coating | Overexposure risk | Very slow drying, fogging risk |

**Optimal working humidity:** 50-60% RH[^10]

[^10]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Going Beyond Blue to Explore Cyanotype Variables: Dilution, Exposure, and Moisture. Part 1](https://www.alternativephotography.com/part-1-cyanotype-dilution-exposure/)

**Low humidity workflow adjustment:**
In dry climates (<40% RH), practitioners report needing **12-15 minutes exposure instead of 8 minutes** to achieve the same blue density. The working hypothesis (supported by Mike Ware) is that low water content slows electron transfer during exposure.[^11]

[^11]: [Photrio.com - Traditional Cyanotype - Effect of Humidity (I think) on Sensitivity](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/traditional-cyanotype-effect-of-humidity-i-think-on-sensitivity.180253/)

### Safe Handling

**Potassium ferricyanide:**
- Stable compound, low toxicity under normal conditions
- Only becomes hazardous if heated beyond 300°F (150°C) or combined with strong acids (releases cyanide gas)
- Do not ingest, inhale dust, or allow skin contact
- **CDC Hazard Evaluation:** Minimal risk with proper handling[^12]

[^12]: [CDC NIOSH - Health Hazard Evaluation Report HETA 81-171-880 CYANOTYPE PRINTING](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/hhe/reports/pdfs/81-171-880.pdf)

**Ferric ammonium citrate:**
- Low toxicity, non-carcinogenic
- Avoid ingestion and prolonged skin contact
- Can cause mild skin irritation in sensitive individuals

**General safety:**
- Work in ventilated area
- Wear nitrile gloves during mixing and coating
- Wash hands thoroughly after handling
- Keep chemistry away from food preparation areas
- Label all containers clearly

### Oxidation Behavior

Unexposed cyanotype coating oxidizes slowly in air, especially in high humidity, causing:
- Gradual shift from yellow-green to pale blue
- Reduced sensitivity (fogging)
- Uneven tonal response

**Mitigation:**
- Coat paper 30-60 minutes before exposure for best results
- If storing coated paper, use airtight bag with silica gel desiccant
- Discard coated paper showing premature blueing

## 3. Paper Selection

### Cotton Rag vs. Wood Pulp Papers

**100% Cotton Rag Papers:**
- **Archival stability:** pH-neutral, no lignin degradation
- **Coating absorption:** Deep penetration, even sensitizer distribution
- **Texture retention:** Holds detail well
- **Cost:** Expensive (€2-5 per sheet for 8×10")
- **Recommended for:** Fine art editions, museum-quality work

**High Alpha Cellulose Papers:**
- **Archival stability:** Good (acid-free, minimal lignin)
- **Coating absorption:** Moderate penetration
- **Cost:** Mid-range (€0.50-2 per sheet)
- **Recommended for:** Serious work, learning workflows

**Wood Pulp Papers:**
- **Archival stability:** Poor (acidic, degrades over decades)
- **Cost:** Very low (€0.10-0.30 per sheet)
- **Recommended for:** Experimentation, testing, learning

### Hot Press vs. Cold Press vs. Rough

| Surface | Texture | Coating Behavior | Detail Rendering | Best For |
|---------|---------|------------------|------------------|----------|
| **Hot Press** | Smooth, minimal texture | Even absorption, thin coating | Sharp detail, fine lines | Botanical contact prints, architectural subjects |
| **Cold Press** | Moderate texture, "tooth" | Uneven absorption in valleys | Softer detail, atmospheric | Landscapes, expressive work |
| **Rough** | Heavy texture, pronounced grain | Very uneven coating | Impressionistic rendering | Large-scale atmospheric work |

**Practitioner consensus:** Most cyanotype printers prefer **hot press** surfaces for control and detail retention, though cold press can enhance atmospheric landscape work.[^13]

[^13]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Papers to use for cyanotypes](https://www.alternativephotography.com/papers-to-use-for-cyanotypes/)

### Sizing and Absorption

**Internal sizing (gelatin or synthetic):**
- Limits sensitizer absorption into paper fibers
- Creates surface coating that can be scratched or abraded
- Advantages: Richer blues, smoother tones
- Disadvantages: Tacky when humid, can damage negatives

**External sizing (surface treatment):**
- Moderates absorption without creating surface film
- Less tacky, safer for negative contact
- More common in watercolor papers

**Unsized papers:**
- Deep penetration of sensitizer (wasteful)
- Weaker blue color (chemistry sinks into fibers)
- Difficult to achieve even coating

### Recommended Papers

Based on extensive practitioner testing and archival documentation:[^14][^15]

| Paper | Weight | Content | Surface | Cost | Best For |
|-------|--------|---------|---------|------|----------|
| **Arches Platine** | 310 gsm | 100% cotton | Hot press | High | Fine art editions, archival work |
| **Hahnemühle Biblio** | 150 gsm | 100% alpha cellulose | Hot press | Medium | Serious work, good value |
| **Stonehenge Light** | 135 gsm | 100% cotton | Smooth | Medium | Learning, testing, editions |
| **Fabriano Unica** | 250 gsm | 50% cotton | Hot press | Medium-Low | Experimentation, testing |
| **Canson XL Watercolor** | 300 gsm | Wood pulp | Cold/Hot | Low | Learning, experimentation |

[^14]: [Photrio.com - Paper for Cyanotypes - An (Incomplete) Survey](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/paper-for-cyanotypes-an-incomplete-survey.175743/)
[^15]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Papers for alternative processes – survey results](https://www.alternativephotography.com/big-paper-survey-results/)

**Beginner recommendation:** Start with inexpensive cartridge or Canson XL papers. Expensive archival papers inhibit experimentation and learning. Move to cotton rag papers once workflow is refined.[^13]

### Texture Effects

Paper texture interacts with coating application:
- **Smooth papers:** Show brush marks prominently unless carefully applied
- **Textured papers:** Hide brush marks, create atmospheric grain
- **Heavy texture:** Can trap sensitizer in valleys, causing uneven exposure

**Artistic consideration:** Some printers embrace visible brush strokes as part of the cyanotype aesthetic, showing the hand-coating process. Others prefer invisible technique. Neither approach is wrong—it's an intentional aesthetic choice.

## 4. Coating Techniques

### Brush Application

**Technique:**[^16]
1. Pour 2-3 ml mixed sensitizer into center of paper
2. Brush **vertically** in smooth, even strokes from top to bottom
3. Brush **horizontally** in smooth, even strokes from left to right
4. Repeat vertical and horizontal passes until evenly covered (3-4 total passes)
5. Work rapidly to prevent uneven drying during application

[^16]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Coating paper by floating, rod or brush](https://www.alternativephotography.com/coating-paper-by-floating-rod-or-brush/)

**Brush selection:**
- **Platinum/palladium "magic brush":** Richeson 9010 (2" wide, soft synthetic)
- **Smooth paper:** Buckle's brush or similar firm synthetic
- **Rough/porous paper:** Soft natural hair brush (hake brush)
- **Sponge brush:** Wide foam brush for fast, even coverage[^17]

[^17]: [Large Format Photography Forum - Where to purchase brush and rod for coating paper](https://www.largeformatphotography.info/forum/archive/index.php/t-119391.html)

**Artistic brush stroke aesthetic:**
Some printers embrace visible brush marks as evidence of hand-coating. This is an intentional artistic choice, not a failure of technique. If you want invisible coating, use glass rod method instead.

### Glass Rod Application

**Technique:**[^16]
1. Use dropper or syringe to draw a **2 ml line of sensitizer** along top edge of paper
2. Place glass rod behind the line
3. Gently draw rod down the paper in one smooth motion, floating it lightly on the surface
4. Lift rod, skip over the sensitizer line at bottom, push rod back to top
5. Repeat 2-3 passes until evenly coated

**Rod diameter:**
- Small prints (8×10" and smaller): 6-8mm diameter glass rod
- Large prints (11×14" and larger): 10-12mm diameter glass rod

**Advantages:**
- Most even coating possible
- Minimal brush marks
- Precise control of coating thickness
- Professional appearance

**Learning curve:** Glass rod technique requires practice. Beginners often apply too much pressure, creating uneven coating. The rod should "float" on the liquid, not scrape the paper.

### Sponge Brush Application

**Technique:**[^18]
1. Mix 10 ml total working solution in wide container
2. Use wide sponge brush (2-3" width preferred)
3. Apply thin, even coating in horizontal strokes
4. Fewer passes = more even coating
5. Work rapidly to prevent uneven drying

[^18]: [National Science and Media Museum - Introduction to the cyanotype process](https://blog.scienceandmediamuseum.org.uk/introduction-cyanotype-process/)

**Best for:** Beginners, large sheets, fabric coating

**Limitation:** Sponge brushes can leave faint texture if coating is too thick

### Coating Consistency and Coverage

**Application amount:**
- **Thin coating:** 0.5-1 ml per 8×10" sheet → pale blue, lower Dmax
- **Standard coating:** 1.5-2 ml per 8×10" sheet → rich blue, good Dmax
- **Double coating:** Two applications (dry between coats) → maximum density, increased contrast

**Working on a molecular level:** You are coating a very thin layer of light-sensitive iron salts. Excessive coating wastes chemistry and creates tacky, uneven surfaces. Thin, even coating is better than thick, blotchy coating.[^18]

### Drying Behavior

**Drying time:** 1 hour to 24 hours depending on:
- Relative humidity (high humidity = slow drying)
- Temperature (warm = fast drying)
- Paper thickness (thick = slow drying)
- Coating thickness (thick = slow drying)

**Optimal drying:**
- Air dry in darkness or dim yellow/red safelight
- Horizontal drying (prevents pooling)
- Good air circulation (fan at low speed)
- 50-60% RH for best results

**High humidity workaround:** Use hairdryer on **cool setting** to accelerate drying and prevent premature blueing.[^19]

[^19]: [Photrio.com - Cyanotype: basic process tutorial](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/cyanotype-basic-process-tutorial.11276/)

### Avoiding Blotchy Coatings

**Common causes and solutions:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| **White specks** | Dust particles in solution | Filter solution before coating |
| **Uneven blue density** | Inadequate mixing of A+B | Mix thoroughly, use within 30 minutes |
| **Blotchy absorption** | Inconsistent paper sizing | Pre-wet paper in distilled water, dry, then coat |
| **Premature blueing** | High humidity during drying | Lower humidity, use hairdryer on cool |
| **Streaks** | Coating drying mid-application | Work faster, use more sensitizer |

## 5. Exposure Methods

### Sunlight Exposure

**Advantages:**
- Free, abundant UV source
- Traditional aesthetic
- Large-format printing possible

**Disadvantages:**
- Inconsistent exposure times (cloud cover, time of day, season)
- Difficult to establish repeatable workflow
- Weather-dependent

**Timing guidelines (approximate):**
- **Bright sun, midday, summer:** 5-15 minutes
- **Hazy sun, midday:** 15-30 minutes
- **Overcast day:** 45-90 minutes

**Visual exposure indicator:**
Properly exposed cyanotype appears **purple and somewhat shimmering** in shadow areas before washing. Overexposed prints shift toward **bronzed green**. Underexposed prints wash out to pale blue.[^20]

[^20]: [Jonah Calinawan - Nail Your Base Exposure Time for Cyanotype](https://jonahcalinawan.com/blog/nail-your-base-exposure-time-for-cyanotype/)

### UV Lamp Exposure

**LED UV Lamps (365nm):**
- **50W LED floodlight (365nm):** 1-2 minutes exposure (New Cyanotype), 8-15 minutes (Traditional)
- **100W LED floodlight (365nm):** 1 minute exposure (New Cyanotype), 5-10 minutes (Traditional)
- **SMD5050 LED strips (365nm):** 10-20 minutes exposure (Traditional)

**Advantages:**
- Consistent, repeatable exposure times
- Indoor workflow year-round
- Predictable results

**Disadvantages:**
- Initial equipment cost (€30-150)
- Requires electricity
- Smaller exposure area than sunlight

**Exposure unit construction:**[^21]
1. Line cardboard box with aluminum foil (reflective interior)
2. Mount LED floodlight at top of box
3. Place contact frame 12-18" below light source
4. Test exposure times with step wedge

[^21]: [Made By Barb - Make an Easy Indoor Exposure Light for Cyanotype Printing](https://www.madebybarb.com/2024/08/25/make-an-easy-indoor-exposure-light-for-cyanotype-printing/)

### Contact Printing Workflow

Cyanotype is a **contact printing process**: the print will be the same size as the negative.

**Contact frame setup:**
1. Place coated paper face-up in contact frame
2. Position negative emulsion-side down on coated paper
3. Close frame back with glass pressing negative into firm contact
4. Expose to UV light

**Negative-to-paper contact quality:**
Poor contact creates soft, out-of-focus details. Traditional cyanotype's tacky surface can damage film negatives. Mike Ware's New Cyanotype formula solves this by absorbing sensitizer into paper, eliminating tackiness.[^7]

### Determining Base Exposure Time

**Base exposure time:** Minimum exposure producing the darkest cyanotype possible for your specific workflow (light source, chemistry, paper, humidity).[^20]

**Testing method:**
1. Coat test strip with working sensitizer
2. Expose in 1-minute increments (LED) or 5-minute increments (sun)
3. Create step wedge: cover 1/5 of strip, expose 1 min, cover another 1/5, expose 1 min, repeat
4. Wash test strip thoroughly
5. Identify darkest blue step = base exposure time
6. Add 10-20% for safety margin

**Example test results (50W LED, Traditional cyanotype, 60% RH):**
- 5 minutes: Pale blue
- 8 minutes: Medium blue
- 12 minutes: Dark blue
- 15 minutes: Maximum density (base exposure)
- 20 minutes: No additional density (overexposure)

**Base exposure = 15 minutes for this specific setup**

### Digital Negative Workflow

Modern cyanotype printing often uses digitally-created negatives printed on transparency film, enabling photographers to work with digital images or small-format film negatives.

**Workflow:**[^22][^23]
1. Scan or digitally capture original image
2. Convert to monochrome
3. Invert to negative
4. Adjust contrast curve for cyanotype density range
5. Print on inkjet transparency film

[^22]: [Jonah Calinawan - How to Create a Cyanotype Digital Negative](https://jonahcalinawan.com/blog/cyanotype-digital-negative/)
[^23]: [Freestyle Photo - How to Make Digital Negatives](https://www.freestylephoto.com/alternative-process/making-digital-negatives)

**Ink color for UV blocking:**
Any ink color works if it blocks UV effectively. **Photo Black** has highest UV density, but **Yellow** is the strongest UV absorber after black. Increasing yellow channel makes negatives more contrasty.[^23]

**Double-negative technique:**
Print two identical negatives and align them precisely in contact frame. This doubles UV density, creating much richer shadows and greater tonal range—useful for negatives that are too thin.[^23]

### Negative Density Requirements

| Formula | Density Range Required | Equivalent Contrast Grade |
|---------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Traditional Cyanotype** | 2.0-2.5 log | #4-5 (very contrasty) |
| **New Cyanotype (Mike Ware)** | 1.6 log | #0 (low contrast) |

**Translation:**
- Traditional cyanotype needs **high-contrast negatives** (density range 2.0-2.5)
- New Cyanotype needs **low-contrast negatives** (density range 1.6)
- Normal negatives for silver gelatin printing (density range 1.0-1.2) will print flat and muddy in traditional cyanotype[^24]

[^24]: [Photrio.com - Fine tuning simple cyanotype to your negatives](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/fine-tuning-simple-cyanotype-to-your-negatives.201037/)

### Contrast Control

**Increasing contrast (traditional cyanotype):**
1. **Add potassium dichromate** (1-2 drops of 1% solution per 100ml sensitizer) → increases contrast in shadows
2. **Double coating** → increases Dmax, raises overall contrast
3. **Curve adjustment in digital negative** → compress midtones, expand shadows/highlights

**Decreasing contrast:**
1. **Add glacial acetic acid** (1-5 ml per 1 liter wash water) → lowers contrast slightly
2. **Expand midtones** in digital negative curve adjustment[^25]

[^25]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Digital negatives](https://www.alternativephotography.com/digital-negatives/)

**Photoshop curve technique for "punch":**
Raise contrast in shadows, lower contrast in midtones. This creates visual separation without blocking highlights.[^25]

## 6. Tonal Control and Print Character

### Weak vs. Rich Cyanotype

**Weak cyanotype characteristics:**
- Pale, washed-out blue
- Low Dmax (maximum density)
- Thin shadows, weak midtones
- Overall flat appearance

**Causes:**
- Underexposure
- Expired or contaminated chemistry
- Excessive dilution of sensitizer
- Insufficient washing (residual sensitizer blocking full color development)

**Rich cyanotype characteristics:**
- Deep, saturated Prussian blue
- High Dmax
- Full tonal range from white highlights to near-black shadows
- Three-dimensional depth

**How to achieve:**
- Optimal exposure (test for base exposure time)
- Fresh, properly mixed chemistry
- Standard or double coating
- Thorough washing (20 minutes minimum)

### Overexposure vs. Underexposure

| Exposure | Appearance Before Wash | Appearance After Wash | Shadow Detail | Highlight Detail |
|----------|------------------------|------------------------|---------------|------------------|
| **Underexposed** | Pale yellow-green | Pale blue, washed out | Lost | Retained (but weak) |
| **Optimal** | Purple, shimmering | Rich blue, full tonal range | Retained | Retained |
| **Overexposed** | Bronzed green | Darker blue, blocked highlights | Retained (too dark) | Lost |

### Highlight Retention

Cyanotype has excellent **shadow retention** but can lose **highlight detail** easily with overexposure or high-contrast negatives.

**Negative density strategy:**
- Ensure negative has adequate density in highlight areas (thin negative = lost highlights)
- For critical highlight detail, expose for highlights and accept deeper shadows

### Shadow Blocking

**Traditional cyanotype** has limited density range (1.0-1.2 log). Negatives with excessive shadow density will render as uniform dark blue without separation.

**Solutions:**
1. Use New Cyanotype formula (2.4 log range)
2. Reduce negative contrast in shadow areas
3. Use development compensation (reduce film development by 1 stop = N-1)

### Humidity Influence on Tonal Rendering

| Humidity Level | Midtone Rendering | Grain/Smoothness | Color Saturation |
|----------------|-------------------|------------------|------------------|
| **<40% (Low)** | Compressed, harsh | Grainy, rough | Lower saturation |
| **50-60% (Ideal)** | Smooth gradation | Smooth, fine grain | Rich saturation |
| **>70% (High)** | Expanded, soft | Smooth, possible fogging | Very saturated |

**Optimal humidity:** 50-60% RH produces smoothest tonal gradations and richest color saturation.[^10]

### Paper Influence on Tonal Rendering

**Hot press papers:**
- Smooth tonal gradations
- Sharp detail
- Maximum Dmax

**Cold press papers:**
- Softer tonal transitions
- Atmospheric rendering
- Slightly lower Dmax

**Cotton rag vs. alpha cellulose:**
Cotton rag papers generally produce richer blues and better tonal separation than alpha cellulose papers due to superior sensitizer absorption and archival chemistry stability.

## 7. Toning and Bleaching

### The Toning Process

Toning a cyanotype involves **two sequential phases**:[^26]

1. **Bleach phase:** Remove Prussian blue by converting ferric ferrocyanide to ferric hydroxide
2. **Toning phase:** Replace ferric hydroxide with ferric tannate (stable, archival compound)

**Critical archival note:** Bleaching without redeveloping in a tannin bath is unstable. Ferric hydroxide degrades over time. Only tannin toning guarantees permanent, archival images.[^26]

[^26]: [AlternativePhotography.com - Toning Cyanotypes](https://www.alternativeprocesses.org/post/toning-cyanotypes)

### Bleaching Agents

| Bleach Type | Preparation | Effect | Archival Safety | Notes |
|-------------|-------------|--------|-----------------|-------|
| **Sodium carbonate (washing soda)** | 50-100 g per liter water | Turns blue to pale yellow-brown | Safe | Preferred method, consistent results |
| **Ammonia solution** | 5-10% household ammonia in water | Turns blue to brown | Safe | Horrible smell, browner results |
| **Chlorine bleach** | AVOID | Bleaches blue rapidly | UNSAFE | Destroys paper fibers, not archival |
| **Soap/detergent** | Dilute alkaline soap | Partial bleaching | Depends on formulation | Inconsistent, not recommended |

**Recommended:** Sodium carbonate (washing soda) is the standard bleaching agent in contemporary alternative process printing.[^26]

### Tannin Sources and Color Range

Every toner contains **tannins** (polyphenolic compounds) that chemically bind to iron in the bleached cyanotype, forming ferric tannate—a permanent, stable compound.[^27]

[^27]: [Made By Barb - How to: Tone Cyanotypes with natural Tannins](https://www.madebybarb.com/2024/07/27/how-to-tone-cyanotypes-with-natural-tannins/)

| Tannin Source | Typical Color Range | Toning Time | Difficulty | Archival Stability |
|---------------|---------------------|-------------|------------|---------------------|
| **Black tea** | Warm brown to sepia | 2-8 hours | Easy | Excellent (ferric tannate) |
| **Green tea** | Pale olive to warm brown | 2-6 hours | Easy | Excellent |
| **Coffee** | Cool brown to chocolate | 1-4 hours | Easy | Excellent |
| **Red wine** | Purple-brown to burgundy | 2-6 hours | Moderate | Excellent |
| **Tannic acid (powder)** | Yellow-brown to near-black | 30 min - 2 hours | Difficult | Excellent |

**Tannic acid warning:** Pure tannic acid powder produces the widest color range (including near-black), but results are unpredictable. Described by practitioners as "a royal pain to work with" with "absolutely no control over what you get."[^28] Colors range from true black to weird purply-brown shades.[^28]

[^28]: [Photrio.com - Tannic Acid Toning a Cyanotype](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/tannic-acid-toning-a-cyanotype.193948/)

### Tea Toning Workflow

**Standard black tea toning:**[^29]

1. **Make print:** Expose and wash cyanotype normally, dry completely
2. **Prepare bleach bath:** 100 g washing soda in 1 liter water
3. **Bleach print:** Immerse print in bleach until blue turns pale yellow-brown (2-5 minutes)
4. **Rinse:** Wash print in running water for 5 minutes to remove bleach
5. **Prepare tea bath:** Steep 10-15 tea bags in 1 liter hot water for 10 minutes, cool to room temperature
6. **Tone print:** Immerse print in tea bath for 2-8 hours (longer = darker brown)
7. **Final wash:** Wash print in running water for 20 minutes
8. **Dry:** Air dry flat

[^29]: [Michelle Vine - TONING CYANOTYPES with TEA](https://michellevine.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ToningWithTea.pdf)

**Time variation:**
- 2 hours: Light warm brown
- 4 hours: Medium sepia brown
- 8 hours: Deep chocolate brown

**Tea toners work really well with minimal bleaching**, making them forgiving for beginners.[^30]

[^30]: [MP Photography - Cyanotype toning: the basics](https://mpaulphotography.wordpress.com/2011/04/01/cyanotype-toning-the-basics/)

### Ammonia Intensification

**Ammonia vapor exposure** (not liquid immersion) can **intensify** blue color in cyanotypes, making pale prints richer. However, ammonia "stinks horribly" and tends to produce browner, less saturated blues.[^31]

[^31]: [Camera Obscura - Ammonia in cyanotype tonings](http://www.co-mag.net/2007/ammonia-cyanotype-toning/)

**Not recommended** except for specific aesthetic effects.

### Split Toning

**Split toning:** Partial bleaching followed by toning creates prints with blue shadows and brown highlights.

**Technique:**
1. Bleach print for only 30-60 seconds (partial bleaching)
2. Rinse quickly
3. Tone in tea or coffee bath
4. Highlights tone brown, shadows remain blue

**Effect:** Atmospheric, painterly quality with warm/cool color contrast

### Archival Implications

**Untoned cyanotype:**
- Archivally stable as Prussian blue (ferric ferrocyanide)
- Resistant to light fading if properly washed
- Sensitive to alkaline environments (degrades in high pH)

**Toned cyanotype:**
- Archivally stable as ferric tannate
- Very permanent, extremely stable over time
- Historical evidence: Dr. Mike Ware reports his family photo albums contain toned cyanotypes that are stable after decades[^26]
- Less sensitive to alkaline degradation than untoned prints

**Critical: Bleached but not toned cyanotypes are NOT archival.** Ferric hydroxide (bleached state) is unstable and degrades. Always complete the toning process.[^26]

### Aesthetic Philosophy

**When to tone:**
- Subject demands warm tones (portraits, intimate landscapes, botanicals)
- Blue feels too cold or clinical for subject matter
- Print needs integration with warm-toned framing or presentation
- Personal aesthetic preference for sepia/brown

**When to leave blue:**
- Subject suits cool, atmospheric tone (water, sky, ice, architecture)
- Prussian blue enhances mood and visual impact
- Historical/technical aesthetic aligns with project concept
- Blue is intentional, not default

**Avoid toning as automatic correction.** Blue cyanotype is not a "problem" requiring a brown "solution." Consider blue and brown as equally valid aesthetic choices, each suited to different subjects and intentions.

## 8. Cyanotype and Film Photography

### Medium Format Negatives (6×4.5, 6×6, 6×7, 6×9)

Medium format negatives (56×56 mm up to 56×84 mm) work well for cyanotype contact printing, producing **intimate-scale prints** suitable for portfolio presentation and smaller wall display.

**Workflow considerations:**
- Contact prints match negative size (6×6 cm = 2.25" × 2.25" print)
- Enlargement requires digital negative workflow
- Detail rendering excellent due to larger film area
- Grain structure minimal at contact print scale

**Suitable subjects:**
- Portraits
- Still life
- Botanical studies
- Intimate landscapes

### Large Format Negatives (4×5, 5×7, 8×10)

Large format film is the **traditional cyanotype printing format**, producing exhibition-scale prints directly from film without digital intermediaries.

**Advantages:**
- Direct contact printing at display size (4×5" = 10×12.5 cm, 8×10" = 20×25 cm)
- Maximum detail and tonal rendering
- No digital negative workflow needed
- Historical aesthetic continuity (Anna Atkins used contact printing)

**Disadvantages:**
- Large format photography requires specialized equipment and expertise
- Film and processing costs significantly higher than 35mm
- Field work more physically demanding

**Contemporary large format cyanotype practice:**[^32]

Many serious cyanotype photographers shoot 4×5" or 8×10" view cameras specifically for alternative printing, avoiding digital negatives entirely. This approach maintains traditional analog workflow from capture through final print.

[^32]: [Photrio.com - CyanoType from Negative Film](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/cyanotype-from-negative-film.114276/)

### Digital Negative Workflow for Small Format

Photographers using 35mm or medium format who want larger cyanotype prints use **digital negative workflow**:

1. **Scan film negative** at high resolution (3200-4800 dpi)
2. **Invert to positive** in photo editing software
3. **Adjust contrast curve** for cyanotype density range (2.0-2.5 log for traditional)
4. **Invert back to negative**
5. **Print on inkjet transparency film** at desired size
6. **Contact print** transparency onto cyanotype paper

**Aesthetic debate:**
Some photographers "wrestle with the idea of a digital component in the middle of a workflow bookended by traditional practice." Others embrace digital negatives as a practical tool enabling large prints from small-format film.[^33]

[^33]: [Analog.cafe - Cyanotype Landscapes: Large Format, Digital Negative, Botanicals](https://www.analog.cafe/r/cyanotype-landscapes-bxp4)

There is no "correct" answer—both approaches have artistic validity.

### Film Development for Cyanotype Printing

**Standard development (Normal, N):**
Produces negatives suitable for silver gelatin printing but often **too low in contrast** for traditional cyanotype.

**High-contrast development strategies:**

| Technique | Development Adjustment | Effect on Negative | Cyanotype Result |
|-----------|------------------------|-------------------|------------------|
| **N+1 development** | +20% development time | Moderate contrast increase | Improved shadow/highlight separation |
| **N+2 development** | +40% development time | High contrast increase | Good for flat-lighting scenes |
| **Lithographic developer** | Special high-contrast developer | Extreme contrast | Risk of blocked highlights |
| **Reduce scanning contrast** | Digital curve adjustment post-scan | Expanded tonal range | Most controlled approach |

**Recommendation:** Develop film normally (N) and adjust contrast during digital negative creation. This preserves maximum tonal information and allows per-image contrast optimization.

### Infrared Negatives

Infrared film (Rollei IR 400, Ilford SFX 200 with R72 filter) creates **surreal, high-contrast negatives** well-suited to cyanotype's aesthetic:

- Foliage renders white (infrared reflectance)
- Sky renders dark (infrared absorption)
- Atmospheric haze penetration
- Dreamlike, otherworldly quality

**Combine infrared film + cyanotype** for distinctive landscape and botanical work.

### Architectural Photography

**Why architecture suits cyanotype:**
- High contrast (bright sky, dark shadows) matches cyanotype's density range
- Prussian blue evokes technical drawing/blueprint heritage
- Sharp detail rendering on hot press paper
- Geometric precision emphasized by contact printing

**Technical approach:**
- Use view camera movements (rise/fall, shift) to correct perspective
- Develop for N+1 to increase contrast
- Print on smooth hot press paper for maximum detail
- Consider leaving blue (toning may weaken architectural impact)

### Landscapes and Atmospheric Photography

**Subject selection:**
- **Water:** Rivers, lakes, oceans (blue reinforces subject)
- **Sky:** Clouds, atmospheric phenomena (dramatic contrast)
- **Winter scenes:** Snow, ice, bare trees (cool tonality)
- **Fog and mist:** Soft gradations, atmospheric depth
- **Mountain landscapes:** Distant haze, tonal perspective

**Avoid:** Direct sunlight, high-key scenes (risk blown highlights)

**Tonal rendering:**
Cyanotype's long tonal scale and smooth gradations excel at rendering atmospheric perspective and subtle tonal shifts in landscape work.

### Botanical Subjects

**Historical precedent:** Anna Atkins' *Photographs of British Algae* established botanical cyanotype as a foundational alternative process subject.

**Modern approach:**
- **Contact printing:** Place botanical specimens directly on coated paper (photogram technique)
- **Film negatives:** Photograph specimens with macro lens, print from negative
- **Pressed flowers/leaves:** Flatten specimens for sharp contact

**Selection criteria:**
- Thin, flat specimens with finely divided outlines (ferns, leaves, grasses)
- Less three-dimensional subjects render better detail
- Translucent specimens create subtle tonal variations

### Long Exposure Photography

Cyanotype pairs naturally with long exposure photography (30 seconds to several minutes) due to:
- Both processes embrace time as material element
- Atmospheric blur and soft water movement suit cyanotype's tonal rendering
- Long exposures increase negative contrast (beneficial for traditional cyanotype)

**Practical workflow:**
Use reciprocity correction for film exposures >1 second (see *Reciprocity Failure Field Guide* for film-specific data). Increased contrast from long exposures can eliminate need for N+1 development.

## 9. Fine Art Cyanotype Philosophy

### Why Cyanotype Works as Wall Art

**Visual impact:**
- **Prussian blue** is a powerful, saturated color that commands attention in gallery spaces
- **Monochrome** focuses viewer on form, texture, light (not distracted by color relationships)
- **Matte surface** (on watercolor paper) reduces glare, inviting close inspection
- **Hand-coated aesthetic** (if brush marks visible) signals craft and artist's hand

**Emotional atmosphere:**
- Cool, contemplative tone
- Associations with water, sky, melancholy, distance
- Historical resonance (Victorian photography, scientific documentation)
- Sense of permanence and archival stability

**Physical presence:**
Cyanotype on heavy cotton rag paper has tactile, object-like quality distinct from photographic prints on resin-coated paper. The paper itself becomes part of the artwork.

### Subject-Process Pairing

**Well-suited subjects:**

| Subject Category | Why It Works | Aesthetic Effect |
|------------------|--------------|------------------|
| **Water (rivers, lakes, oceans)** | Blue reinforces subject, atmospheric quality | Unified color-subject relationship |
| **Botanical studies** | Historical precedent (Atkins), blue evokes scientific specimen | Contemplative, archival documentation |
| **Architecture** | Blueprint heritage, geometric precision | Technical, structural emphasis |
| **Winter landscapes** | Cool tonality matches subject temperature | Coherent mood and color relationship |
| **Atmospheric phenomena** | Long tonal scale renders subtle gradations | Soft, meditative quality |

**Poorly suited subjects:**
- **Warm-toned subjects** (sunsets, autumn foliage, fire) → blue fights subject
- **Portraits** (unless intentionally cool/surreal) → skin tones render unnaturally
- **High-key, bright scenes** → risk losing highlight detail
- **Complex color relationships** → monochrome eliminates color as compositional element

**Exception:** Toning transforms cyanotype into warm browns/sepias, opening subjects like portraits and autumn scenes. But toning should be intentional choice, not automatic correction.

### Avoiding Cliché Imagery

**Cyanotype clichés to avoid:**
- Generic ferns and leaves (overdone since Anna Atkins)
- Predictable water/sky landscapes without compositional depth
- "Blue = cyanotype" as the only concept
- Surface-level prettiness without conceptual foundation

**How to avoid cliché:**
- **Conceptual depth:** What is the image about beyond visual appearance?
- **Personal vision:** What unique perspective do you bring?
- **Formal rigor:** Is the composition strong independent of process?
- **Subject-process integration:** Why does this subject need to be a cyanotype?

**Test:** Would this image work as a silver gelatin print? If yes, why make it a cyanotype? If the answer is only "because blue looks nice," reconsider.

### Framing and Presentation

**Museum-standard framing:**
- **Mat:** Acid-free, 100% cotton rag mat board (pH 8.5-9.5)
- **Backing:** Acid-free foam core or archival corrugated board
- **Glass:** UV-filtering glass or acrylic (blocks 97-99% UV radiation)
- **Frame:** Wood or metal frame with proper sealing

**Critical: Use UV-filtering glass.** Cyanotypes fade with continued UV exposure. Regular glass blocks some UV but not enough for long-term protection.[^34]

[^34]: [Techy Life - How Long Do Cyanotype Prints Last? Unveiling the Longevity of These Striking Photographic Gems](https://thetechylife.com/how-long-do-cyanotype-prints-last/)

**Alkaline mat board warning:** Avoid alkaline-buffered mat boards (pH >9.5). High alkalinity degrades Prussian blue over time. Use pH-neutral or slightly alkaline boards (pH 8.5-9.0).[^35]

[^35]: [University of Illinois PSAP - Preservation Self-Assessment Program: Monochrome Photographs (Cyanotype)](https://psap.library.illinois.edu/advanced-help/photo-monochrome-cyanotype)

**Alternative presentation:**
- **Hinge mounting:** Allows print to float on backing, showing paper edges
- **Japanese-style mounting:** Dry-mounting on archival board, no glass
- **Portfolio boxes:** Unmounted prints stored flat in archival boxes (best for editions)

### Edition Consistency

**Challenge:** Hand-coating cyanotypes introduces variation between prints. Achieving identical editions requires rigorous process control.

**Controlling variables:**
1. **Chemistry:** Use same batch of mixed sensitizer for entire edition
2. **Paper:** Use same paper stock, same manufacturer lot
3. **Coating:** Measure sensitizer volume precisely, use identical coating technique
4. **Exposure:** Use UV lamp with timer (not sunlight), establish base exposure for this edition
5. **Washing:** Same water temperature, same wash duration for all prints
6. **Drying:** Same drying method and environment

**Practical reality:** Perfect edition consistency is very difficult in cyanotype. Some variation is inherent to hand-coating processes. Many artists embrace subtle variation as evidence of handcraft rather than fighting for photographic consistency.

**Artistic decision:** Do you want photographic consistency (identical prints) or handcraft variation (each print unique)? Both are valid approaches with different aesthetic implications.

### Contemporary Cyanotype Practice

**Notable contemporary cyanotype photographers:**[^36]
- **Anna Leigh Clem** – Atmospheric landscapes toned with foraged botanicals
- **Kate Cordsen** – Abstract cyanotypes suspended between landscape and abstraction
- **Andy Farrer** – Landscape cyanotypes exploring atmospheric phenomena
- **Michelle Vine** – Botanical and landscape work with traditional aesthetics

[^36]: [Analog Forever Magazine - The Largest Curated Gallery of Cyanotypes of 2021](https://www.analogforevermagazine.com/online-exhibitions/certain-kind-of-blue-march-2021)

**Trends:**
- Integration of digital and analog workflows (digital negatives)
- Toning for expanded color palette
- Experimental substrates (glass, ceramic, fabric)
- Conceptual approaches beyond literal representation

## 10. Troubleshooting

### Uneven Coating

| Symptom | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|----------------|----------|
| **White specks throughout print** | Dust particles in sensitizer | Filter solution through coffee filter before coating |
| **Streaky, uneven blue density** | Coating dried during application | Work faster, use more sensitizer, increase humidity |
| **Blotchy, mottled appearance** | Inconsistent paper sizing | Pre-wet paper in distilled water, dry completely, then coat |
| **Visible brush marks** | Heavy-handed brush application | Use glass rod technique, or embrace brush marks as aesthetic |
| **Edge pooling** | Excess sensitizer, uneven coating | Use measured sensitizer amount (1.5-2ml per 8×10"), work on level surface |

**Dust-free environment:** Work in cleanest available space. Wipe work surface with damp cloth before coating. Strain chemistry through fine filter.[^37]

[^37]: [AlternativeProcesses.org - Troubleshooting Common Cyanotype Issues](https://www.alternativeprocesses.org/post/troubleshooting-common-cyanotype-issues-why-your-prints-may-appear-too-light-dark-or-uneven)

### Weak Blue Color

| Symptom | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|----------------|----------|
| **Pale, washed-out blue after washing** | Underexposure | Increase exposure time 50%, retest |
| **Weak blue despite long exposure** | Expired or contaminated chemistry | Mix fresh batch, ensure distilled water |
| **Uneven weak areas** | Uneven coating thickness | Improve coating technique, ensure even coverage |
| **Progressive weakening over multiple prints** | Mixed sensitizer degrading | Mix fresh working solution (A+B mixture degrades after 2-3 weeks) |

**Double-coating technique:** Apply first coat, dry completely, apply second coat. This increases Dmax and overall saturation significantly.[^38]

[^38]: [Photrio.com - How to improve density in Cyanotypes?](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/how-to-improve-density-in-cyanotypes.175947/)

### Staining

| Symptom | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|----------------|----------|
| **Overall blue fog in highlights** | Premature exposure during drying | Dry in complete darkness or under red/yellow safelight |
| **Yellow-brown staining in highlights** | Incomplete washing, residual iron salts | Wash thoroughly for 20 minutes minimum in running water |
| **Uneven blue splotches** | Contaminated water during coating or washing | Use only distilled/deionized water for mixing and washing |
| **Dark spots or specks** | Iron contamination from tap water | Filter all water, use glass or plastic containers (not metal) |

**Alkaline contamination:** Even trace amounts of alkaline chemicals (soap residue on brushes, alkaline paper) bleach cyanotype highlights. Dedicate brushes exclusively to cyanotype, never use with alkaline materials.[^39]

[^39]: [Photrio.com - Cyanotype highlight staining help!](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/cyanotype-highlight-staining-help.157793/)

### Fogging

| Fogging Type | Cause | Solution |
|--------------|-------|----------|
| **Heat fogging** | Excessive drying temperature | Air dry at room temperature, avoid heat |
| **Light fogging** | Light exposure during coating/drying | Work in dim yellow/red safelight, load negatives in low light |
| **Radiation fogging** | Cosmic radiation (extremely rare) | Not applicable to amateur practice |
| **Moisture fogging** | High humidity during drying | Lower humidity, use hairdryer on cool setting |

**Citric acid fog reduction:** Add 1 drop of 40% citric acid solution to coating mix (never add directly to stock bottles). This can reduce fog slightly.[^40]

[^40]: [Photrio.com - Cyanotype Fogging](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/cyanotype-fogging.202004/)

### Contamination

**Water contamination:**
Tap water contains minerals (iron, calcium, magnesium) that contaminate cyanotype chemistry. **Always use distilled or deionized water** for:
- Mixing stock solutions
- Mixing working solution
- Final washing

**Paper contamination:**
Some papers contain chemicals (brighteners, sizing agents, residual bleach) that react with cyanotype sensitizer. If unexplained staining or weak results occur, try different paper brand.[^41]

[^41]: [Photrio.com - Cyanotype already blue before exposure](https://www.photrio.com/forum/threads/cyanotype-already-blue-before-exposure.175528/)

**Brush contamination:**
Dedicate brushes exclusively to cyanotype. Never use brushes previously used with:
- Alkaline chemicals (detergent, soap, washing soda)
- Oil-based media (will repel water-based sensitizer)
- Acidic fixers (can contaminate chemistry)

### Poor Contrast

| Symptom | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|----------------|----------|
| **Muddy, flat print** | Low-contrast negative | Use N+1 film development or adjust digital negative curve |
| **Blocked highlights, empty shadows** | Excessive contrast in negative | Reduce negative contrast, use New Cyanotype formula (longer density range) |
| **Overall flat despite contrasty negative** | Underexposure or weak chemistry | Increase exposure time, mix fresh chemistry |

**Negative density testing:** Use densitometer or visual inspection to ensure negative density range matches cyanotype requirements (2.0-2.5 log for traditional formula).

### Muddy Shadows

**Cause:** Excessive shadow density in negative. Traditional cyanotype cannot separate tones in very dense negative areas.

**Solutions:**
1. **Reduce shadow density** in digital negative (raise black point slightly)
2. **Use New Cyanotype formula** (2.4 log density range vs. 1.2 log for traditional)
3. **N-1 film development** (reduce development time by 20%) to lower shadow density

### Paper Issues

| Issue | Cause | Solution |
|-------|-------|----------|
| **Coating beads up, doesn't absorb** | Heavily sized paper | Pre-wet paper, dry, then coat; or switch to less-sized paper |
| **Coating absorbs too quickly** | Unsized or lightly sized paper | Use more sensitizer, work faster; or switch to better-sized paper |
| **Paper curls during washing** | Thin paper (<200 gsm) | Use heavier paper (300 gsm+), or soak in water before coating |
| **Paper tears during washing** | Weak, low-quality paper | Upgrade to cotton rag or high alpha cellulose paper |

### Humidity Problems

**Too low (<40% RH):**
- Coating difficult to spread evenly
- Exposure time 1.5-2× longer than normal
- Grainy, rough tonal rendering

**Solution:** Use humidifier in workspace, wet sponge in exposure box, work during more humid seasons

**Too high (>70% RH):**
- Coating doesn't dry, remains tacky
- Premature blueing (fogging)
- Risk of mold growth in stock solutions

**Solution:** Use dehumidifier, hairdryer on cool setting for drying, add silica gel desiccant to storage

## 12. Archival Storage and Presentation

### Washing Requirements

**Thoroughness of washing directly affects archival stability.** Residual sensitizer causes progressive fading and staining over time.[^42]

[^42]: [Cultural Heritage Resources - A Blueprint for Conserving Cyanotypes](https://resources.culturalheritage.org/pmgtopics/2003-volume-ten/10_02_Ware.html)

**Water type comparison:**[^42]

| Water Type | pH | Image Density Loss | Archival Recommendation |
|------------|----|--------------------|-------------------------|
| **Tap water** | 7.5-8.5 | 18% loss | Not recommended (minerals contaminate) |
| **Distilled water** | 6.0-6.5 | 4% loss | Good |
| **Deionized water** | 6.3-6.6 | 0% loss | Best |

**Washing duration:**[^42]
- **4 minutes:** Maximum fade of 42% under light exposure (insufficient)
- **15 minutes:** 18% density loss (minimal acceptable)
- **20 minutes:** Maximum fade of 16% (good)
- **30 minutes:** Archival standard (best)

**Recommended washing procedure:**
1. Remove print from contact frame immediately after exposure
2. Place in water bath (room temperature, 20-25°C)
3. Agitate gently for first 5 minutes (watch blue develop)
4. Change water every 5 minutes
5. Continue washing for **minimum 20 minutes total** (30 minutes for archival work)
6. Final rinse in deionized water for 5 minutes

**Visual indicator:** Print is fully washed when water runs completely clear and no yellow tint remains in highlights.

### Drying

**Air drying (recommended):**[^34]
- Lay print flat on clean, lint-free surface (fiberglass screen, plastic mesh, clean towel)
- Dry in dim light or darkness
- Ensure good air circulation (fan at low speed helps)
- Avoid direct sunlight (begins UV exposure/fading immediately)
- Drying time: 2-24 hours depending on humidity and paper thickness

**Avoid heat drying:** Heat drying (hair dryer on hot, oven, radiator) can cause:
- Uneven drying and warping
- Accelerated chemical reactions (unpredictable tonal shifts)
- Paper embrittlement

**Cool hairdryer exception:** Hairdryer on **cool setting** is acceptable for speeding drying in high-humidity environments, as long as heat is not applied.

### Flattening

Heavy watercolor papers curl during washing and drying. Flatten prints using:

**Dry mounting press (professional):**
- Low temperature (180-200°F / 80-95°C)
- 30-60 seconds under pressure
- Interleave with silicone release paper

**Weight pressing (home method):**
1. Place dry print between blotters or clean newsprint
2. Stack heavy books on top (20-30 lbs / 10-15 kg total weight)
3. Leave for 24-48 hours
4. Replace damp blotters after 12 hours if needed

**Humidity chamber (archival method):**
1. Expose print to high humidity (80-90% RH) for 10-15 minutes to relax fibers
2. Immediately place between blotters under weight
3. Press for 24 hours as above

### UV Resistance and Fading

**Cyanotypes are sensitive to light** and will fade with continued UV exposure, even behind glass.[^34]

**Fading behavior:**
- **Direct intense light:** Rapid fading (weeks to months)
- **Indirect daylight:** Moderate fading (years)
- **Dark storage:** Minimal fading (decades to centuries)

**Critical protective measure: UV-filtering glass**[^34]

| Glazing Type | UV Transmission | Archival Suitability |
|--------------|-----------------|----------------------|
| **Regular glass** | 50-70% UV passes | Poor (fading occurs) |
| **UV-filtering glass (Museum Glass, TruVue)** | <3% UV passes (97-99% blocked) | Excellent |
| **UV-filtering acrylic (Plexiglas UF-3, UF-5)** | <3% UV passes | Excellent |

**Cost trade-off:** UV-filtering glass costs 2-3× more than regular glass, but essential for archival preservation.

**Self-healing property:**
Fascinatingly, **faded cyanotypes can regain substantial image density when kept in dark storage**. This reversible fading is unique to Prussian blue chemistry.[^43] However, repeated fade/recovery cycles eventually cause permanent degradation—prevent fading rather than relying on recovery.

[^43]: [University of Illinois PSAP - Preservation Self-Assessment Program: Photographic Prints](https://psap.library.illinois.edu/collection-id-guide/photoprint)

### Framing Behind UV Glass

**Museum-standard framing:**[^35]

1. **Mat:** 4-ply, 100% cotton rag mat board, pH 8.5-9.0 (NOT alkaline-buffered >pH 9.5)
2. **Hinging:** Attach print to mat with archival linen tape or Japanese tissue hinges (never dry-mount or use pressure-sensitive tape)
3. **Glazing:** UV-filtering glass or acrylic (TruVue Museum Glass, Tru Vue Conservation Clear)
4. **Backing:** Acid-free foam core or archival corrugated board
5. **Frame:** Sealed wood or metal frame with dust cover on back

**Critical alkaline warning:** **Avoid alkaline-buffered mat boards** (pH >9.5, often labeled "acid-free buffered"). High alkalinity degrades Prussian blue over time. Use pH-neutral or slightly alkaline boards (pH 8.5-9.0).[^35]

**Why this matters:** Calcium carbonate buffering (common in conservation boards) creates pH 9.5-10.5 environments that slowly bleach cyanotype prints. This is a well-documented conservation issue.[^35]

### Storage Conditions

**Optimal environment:**[^35]
- **Temperature:** 65-70°F (18-21°C)
- **Relative humidity:** 30-50% RH (avoid high humidity)
- **Light:** Dark storage (no light exposure)
- **Air quality:** Clean, pollutant-free (avoid industrial areas, vehicle exhaust)

**Enclosure requirements:**[^35]
- **Material:** Acid-free (pH 7.2-9.5), **unbuffered** paper or uncoated plastic (polyester, polyethylene, polypropylene)
- **Individual enclosures:** Each print in its own sleeve/envelope/wrapper (prevents abrasion)
- **Storage boxes:** Lignin-free archival boxes, unbuffered

**Critical: Use unbuffered storage materials.** Alkaline-buffered enclosures damage cyanotypes.[^35]

**Flat storage:**
- Store prints flat in archival boxes or flat files
- Avoid rolled storage (causes permanent creasing)
- Interleave prints with acid-free tissue (prevents friction)

### Longevity

**Archival lifespan (properly processed and stored):**[^34]
- **Untoned cyanotype:** Centuries (Prussian blue is highly stable)
- **Toned cyanotype:** Centuries (ferric tannate is highly stable)

**Historical evidence:**
- Anna Atkins cyanotypes from 1843 (183 years old) remain vibrant in museum collections
- Victorian blueprint technical drawings from 1850s-1900s survive in archives
- Dr. Mike Ware's family photo albums contain stable toned cyanotypes from decades ago[^26]

**Factors affecting longevity:**
1. **Washing thoroughness:** Incomplete washing → residual iron salts → progressive fading
2. **Storage pH:** Alkaline environment (pH >9.5) → bleaching of Prussian blue
3. **Light exposure:** UV light → fading (even behind regular glass)
4. **Humidity:** High humidity → mold growth, paper degradation
5. **Air pollutants:** Sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides → chemical degradation

**Best-case scenario:** Cyanotype washed for 30 minutes in deionized water, stored in pH-neutral unbuffered enclosures, in dark storage at 65°F and 40% RH, displayed behind UV glass → lifespan of 200-500+ years.

**Worst-case scenario:** Cyanotype washed for 5 minutes in tap water, stored in alkaline-buffered mat, displayed in direct sunlight behind regular glass → fading within 5-10 years.

**Archival stability is a choice, not an accident.** Follow conservation best practices for museum-quality longevity.

## Glossary

**Ammonium iron(III) oxalate** – Iron salt used in Mike Ware's New Cyanotype formula, replaces ferric ammonium citrate for faster exposure and greater density range.

**Base exposure time** – Minimum exposure duration producing maximum cyanotype density (Dmax) for a given workflow (light source, chemistry, paper, humidity). Determined through step wedge testing.

**Bleaching** – Chemical process converting Prussian blue (ferric ferrocyanide) to ferric hydroxide using alkaline bath (sodium carbonate, ammonia). First step in toning process.

**Contact printing** – Photographic printing technique where negative is placed in direct contact with light-sensitive material (cyanotype paper) during exposure. Print size equals negative size.

**Density range** – Difference between maximum density (Dmax, darkest tone) and minimum density (Dmin, lightest tone) in a negative or print, measured in log units. Traditional cyanotype has ~1.0-1.2 log range; New Cyanotype has ~2.4 log range.

**Digital negative** – Digitally created negative image printed on inkjet transparency film, enabling cyanotype printing from digital files or small-format negatives.

**Dmax** – Maximum density (darkest blue) achievable in a cyanotype print. Affected by coating thickness, exposure, washing thoroughness, paper type.

**Ferric ammonium citrate** – Iron(III) salt used in traditional cyanotype formula. Light-sensitive compound that reduces to ferrous state under UV exposure, reacting with potassium ferricyanide to form Prussian blue.

**Ferric ferrocyanide** – Chemical name for Prussian blue, the blue pigment formed in cyanotype printing. Archivally stable compound responsible for characteristic blue color.

**Ferric hydroxide** – Unstable iron compound formed during bleaching step of toning. Must be converted to ferric tannate (via toning) for archival stability.

**Ferric tannate** – Permanent, stable brown compound formed when tannins react with bleached cyanotype. Archivally stable alternative to Prussian blue.

**Fogging** – Unwanted overall blue density in cyanotype highlights caused by premature light exposure, high humidity during drying, or contaminated chemistry.

**Hot press** – Smooth paper surface with minimal texture, created by pressing paper between hot rollers during manufacture. Preferred for cyanotype due to even coating and sharp detail rendering.

**N+1 development** – Film development technique increasing development time by ~20% to raise negative contrast. Useful for negatives intended for cyanotype printing.

**Potassium ferricyanide** – Iron(III) compound (K₃[Fe(CN)₆]) used in cyanotype sensitizer. Reacts with ferrous ions (produced by UV reduction of ferric ammonium citrate) to form Prussian blue.

**Prussian blue** – Intense blue pigment (ferric ferrocyanide) formed in cyanotype process. Discovered 1706, archivally stable, characteristic cyanotype color.

**Reciprocity failure** – Breakdown of linear exposure relationship at very long or very short exposures. Rarely affects cyanotype (exposure times typically 2-30 minutes, well within reciprocity law range).

**Sensitizer** – Light-sensitive chemical mixture (ferric ammonium citrate + potassium ferricyanide for traditional cyanotype) coated on paper before exposure.

**Tannin** – Polyphenolic plant compound (found in tea, coffee, wine, oak galls) that chemically binds to iron, forming stable ferric tannate. Used for toning cyanotypes from blue to brown.

**Toning** – Two-step process: (1) bleaching Prussian blue to ferric hydroxide, (2) converting ferric hydroxide to ferric tannate via tannin bath. Changes cyanotype from blue to brown/sepia tones.

**UV (ultraviolet) light** – Electromagnetic radiation with wavelength 300-400 nm, invisible to human eye. Cyanotype is sensitive to UV and blue-violet light (300-450 nm). Sources include sunlight, UV LED lamps, UV fluorescent tubes.
